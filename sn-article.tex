\abstract{Money mediates earning and spending, and accumulated monetary holdings constitute both a basic form of wealth and an external resource state that shapes economic decisions. Unlike internal physiological states such as hunger or satiety, external resources persist outside the body, can be acquired or depleted through choice, and preserve access to future consumption. Although money and wealth are human constructs, balancing resource acquisition and consumption may rely on evolutionarily conserved neural computations. Here we investigated these computations using an earning–spending task, in which rhesus monkeys chose between earning virtual tokens and spending accumulated tokens for water, with holdings carrying across trials. As holdings varied, monkeys shifted between earning and spending choices. A state-dependent utility model explained this shift through opposing changes in the relative values of earning and spending. We recorded single-unit activities from the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC). Neuronal populations across all three regions represented current holdings, while ACC and OFC updated these representations after earning and spending outcomes. Crucially, resource state modulated value coding and reshaped the geometry of earning- and spending-value representations across all three regions. OFC exhibited a broader spectrum of signals spanning option value, chosen value, and good-based choice, whereas DLPFC representations were weighted more strongly toward action-based choice. Together, these findings identify distributed prefrontal mechanisms that represent, update, and use an external resource state to guide valuation and choice, extending neural accounts of state-dependent valuation beyond internal physiological conditions and suggesting an evolutionarily conserved foundation for balancing resource acquisition and consumption.}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Money is a key intermediary in our everyday economic behavior. People work to acquire money, retain it as wealth, and spend it to obtain goods and experiences. Accumulated monetary holdings therefore constitute an external resource state that shapes these behaviors. Earning an additional unit may be especially valuable when holdings are low but less valuable when they are abundant \cite{bernoulli1954exposition}, whereas spending may become more attractive as resources accumulate. Economic decisions therefore depend not only on the opportunities currently available, but also on the resource state in which those opportunities are evaluated \cite{cesarini2017effect,vanags2025greater,harvey2022developmental}.

This dependence creates a recurring computational problem. An individual must represent current holdings, evaluate opportunities to acquire and consume resources in light of those holdings, and select an option that changes the resource state for subsequent decisions. Resource state thus influences valuation and choice, while earning and spending update the resource state itself \cite{mangel1986unified,mangel1988dynamic}.

This computational problem resembles the state-dependent valuation processes commonly studied in relation to internal physiological states such as hunger, thirst, and satiety \citep{piech2009appetite,yamada2013thirst,pastorbernier2021satiety}. In both cases, current state must be integrated with available opportunities to determine their values \citep{juechems2019value}. External resources, however, differ fundamentally from internal states. They persist outside the body, can be acquired or depleted through choice, and preserve access to future consumption. Although the neural effects of internal motivational states have been studied extensively \citep{encarnacionrivera2025drives,piech2009appetite,bouret2010motivation,pastorbernier2021satiety}, comparatively little is known about how the brain represents, updates, and uses accumulated external resources. Here, we ask whether monetary holding, as an external resource state, modulates valuation and choice through related neural principles.

At the neural level, signals related to offer value, chosen value, and categorical choice have been identified across prefrontal cortex, including the orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) \cite{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b,padoa-schioppaOrbitofrontalCortexNeural2017,ballestaValuesEncodedOrbitofrontal2020a}, the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) \cite{cai2014contributions,lin2021evidence}, and the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) \cite{caiNeuronalEncodingSubjective2012,kennerleyNeuronsFrontalLobe2009b}. Token-based studies have also shown that accumulated holdings influence preferences, including decisions involving gains, losses, and risk, and that neural activity can represent token holdings and other wealth-like reference states \cite{yangPrimateAnteriorInsular2022c,ferroAccumulationVirtualTokens2025a,nguyenNeuralRepresentationDecisional2025a,seo2009behavioral}. These findings identify component signals relevant to resource-dependent choice, but do not explain how they operate together. In particular, it remains unknown how current holdings modulate the values of earning and spending opportunities, how resource-state representations are updated after acquisition and consumption, and how the resulting decision is expressed in good-based and action-based coordinates.

Here we developed a new earning–spending task in which rhesus monkeys chose between acquiring virtual tokens and spending accumulated tokens for water. Tokens carried over across trials, and both earning and spending could continue or stop at intermediate levels of holdings, creating a continuously changing resource state without a fixed redemption threshold. As holdings increased, monkeys shifted systematically from earning toward spending, consistent with opposing state-dependent changes in the relative values of the two options. We combined computational modeling with single-neuron recordings from ACC, OFC, and DLPFC to ask how current resource state alters the relative values of earning and spending, how prefrontal populations represent and update this state, and how state-dependent values are transformed into choices and actions. Together, these findings show how distributed prefrontal representations link accumulated resources to valuation and earning–spending choice.

\subsection{Token holdings shift earning--spending preferences}

Three rhesus macaques were trained on an earning--spending task (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}a) in which they chose between performing a fixation task for virtual tokens and spending accumulated tokens for water rewards. Each trial paired one of three earning options, distinguished by earning rate ($V_\text{Earn}$, tokens/s), with one of four spending options, distinguished by water amount in exchange for a token ($V_\text{Spend}$, a.u.) (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}b). Tokens carried over across trials until spent, so accumulated token holdings constituted a continuous external resource state.

The initial choice on each trial revealed two consistent behavioral patterns (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}c). First, across token holdings, monkeys were more likely to earn when the earning rate was higher and the spending option had less value. This pattern indicates that all three monkeys learned the offer contingencies. Second, within individual offer conditions, the tendency to choose to earn generally declined as tokens accumulated. Thus, choices reflected not only the current offers but also the animal's current token holdings.

This state-dependent shift in choice is consistent with diminishing marginal utility: accumulating tokens reduces the marginal benefit of earning an additional token and the opportunity cost of spending one. To assess the change in valuation without imposing a particular functional form, we estimated non-parametric subjective weights for the seven option stimuli at each of the 24 token levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}d; see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_nonparam}). In all three monkeys, the subjective weights of earning options generally decreased, whereas those of spending options generally increased, as tokens accumulated, showing a state-dependent shift in the relative weights favoring spending over earning.

We next formalized this account using a parametric utility model based on diminishing marginal utility (see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_param}). We assumed a power-law utility function $U(H) = H^\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the curvature exponent, with marginal utility $U'(H)=\alpha H^{\alpha-1}$. The model integrated current token holdings $H$ with the offer attributes to estimate earning and spending values.

Probability of choosing to earn was a sigmoid function of the value difference in all three monkeys (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}e and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}c). Across 150 recording sessions (W, 26; U, 61; T, 63), the free-$\alpha$ model yielded lower held-out loss than the fixed-$\alpha$ model in all three subjects (W, $p=9.34\times10^{-6}$; U, $p=1.92\times10^{-11}$; T, $p=5.17\times10^{-12}$; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}a). Independent session-wise fits characterized the distribution of fitted utility curvature across recording sessions (median $\alpha$: W, 0.72; U, 0.81; T, 0.79; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}b). Moreover, the value difference predicted reaction time: responses were slower near the fitted behavioral indifference point (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}d). Together, these results support a token-holding-dependent earning-spending preference and relate the value difference between the earning and spending options to both choice and reaction time.

\subsection{Distributed prefrontal tuning represents current token holdings}
Having established that current token holdings systematically shifted earning--spending preferences, we next asked how they were represented by individual prefrontal units. A total of 2,790 units were included in the analyses: 715 in ACC, 1,113 in DLPFC and 962 in OFC (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_recording_sites}). We divided the amount of tokens into five approximately logarithmically spaced groups ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15] and [16--24] tokens) to accommodate the compressed scaling commonly observed for numerical magnitude \cite{niederMiller2003compressed,niederDehaene2009number}, while keeping trial counts more comparable across groups. 

Units in all three regions showed significant selectivity for token holdings. Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}a,b shows an OFC unit responding most strongly in the low wealth state, whereas Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}c,d shows a DLPFC unit with maximal firing in the intermediate wealth state. Across ACC, DLPFC and OFC, preferred wealth levels were distributed across all five groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}e and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}a).

We next examined the shape of the units' tuning functions for token holdings. Units preferring different token-holding groups exhibited similarly shaped tuning curves centered at different points along the token-holding axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}f--h). Re-centering the curves on their preferred group revealed a common Gaussian-like profile in all three regions, with firing rates declining progressively as token holdings moved away from the preferred level (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}i--k). The well-aligned Gaussian-like tuning curve was not an artifact: normalized responses were higher at relative distances of $\pm1$ than at $\pm2$ from the preferred group in all three regions (ACC, $p=6.49\times10^{-31}$; DLPFC, $p=2.61\times10^{-32}$; OFC, $p=6.78\times10^{-40}$; two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests), a difference that was not significant after token-holding-group labels were shuffled (all $p\geq0.184$; Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}b). Together, tuning curves of similar shape whose preferred levels tiled the range of token holdings provided a distributed population representation of animals' current token holdings.

\subsection{Temporal dynamics and choice-dependent updating of token-holding representations}
As the animals were constantly earning and spending tokens, the corresponding prefrontal representation of current token holdings should track their dynamics. We therefore applied cross-temporal decoding based on targeted dimensionality reduction (TDR) \cite{mante2013context} (see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_tdr_all}). At each time bin, a TDR-derived token-holding axis was fitted independently and tested at every other bin, yielding a $24 \times 24$ matrix of encoding strength, quantified as squared Pearson correlations ($r^2$) spanning the offer-onset and choice-onset epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}a and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_cross_temporal}). Here, choice onset denotes the wallet update starting an intermediate choice within continuous fixation, with token holdings and other variables assigned to that ensuing choice (see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_events}). During the offer-onset epoch, decoding was strongest near the temporal diagonal, suggesting a dynamic representation of token number. By contrast, decoding generalized broadly across the choice-onset epoch, demonstrating a stable token-holding representation during this period.

Trial averaging in the cross-temporal analysis could obscure the transition structure associated with each earning and spending event. We therefore projected population activity from $-50$ to $150$~ms relative to choice outcome (see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_events}) onto TDR-derived choice and token axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}b). Earning drove activity toward the direction of more tokens in ACC and OFC, whereas spending drove it toward the opposite direction. To quantify these shifts, we regressed the token-holding-axis projection against time separately for earning and spending trials within each resampling iteration. The resulting temporal coefficient measured the direction and rate of movement along the token axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}c and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_state_updating}). We defined choice-dependent token updating as the joint presence of a significantly positive earning coefficient and a significantly negative spending coefficient. In the pooled data, ACC (earning, $p=6.98\times10^{-4}$; spending, $p=9.55\times10^{-10}$) and OFC (earning, $p=6.54\times10^{-17}$; spending, $p=1.81\times10^{-4}$) satisfied this bidirectional criterion, whereas DLPFC did not (earning, $p=8.19\times10^{-3}$; spending, $p=1.12\times10^{-1}$). Neither choice-label nor token-label shuffling yielded bidirectional updating in any region (in each case, at least one coefficient was non-significant, $p\geq0.131$). Thus, ACC and OFC not only represented current token number but also updated that representation when the wallet changed, providing an updated state signal that could influence subsequent value coding and decision making.

\subsection{Token holdings modulate prefrontal offer-value representations}
Having established that prefrontal populations represented and updated the current token holdings, we next asked whether this state was incorporated into the neural processing of earning and spending values. The behavioral model suggested that diminishing marginal utility would reduce earning values as tokens accumulated, whereas spending values would retain the separation attributable to reward magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}a,b). Example OFC neurons exhibited response patterns consistent with the behavioral model (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}a,b).

We used a nested-model comparison to examine state-dependent value encoding beyond the independent effects of offers and holdings. Relative to a baseline model ($M_1$) containing state-independent offer values, categorical token-holding terms and choice, a second model ($M_2$) additionally included the token-holding-dependent earning value $V_E$ derived from the behavioral model (see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_nested}). Around choice onset, the increase in explained variance, $\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}$, was significantly greater than the shuffled-$V_E$ control in ACC, DLPFC and OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}c and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}c). This increment supports a non-additive relationship between earning-offer information and token holdings in single-unit activity around choice onset. An analogous nested-model test was not identifiable for $V_S$ because its state-dependent component was additive with token holdings and was therefore absorbed by the token-holding terms already present in $M_1$ (see Methods, \nameref{sec:m_nested}).

We next used TDR analyses to examine how this state dependence was expressed in a population value space defined by earning- and spending-value axes, plotting the earning-spending conditions as an array (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}d). We quantified the geometry of this representation using the aspect ratio between the spending- and earning-axis ranges. Because the behavioral model predicted that the separation among earning values would narrow while the separation among spending values would be preserved, this ratio was expected to increase with token holdings, visualized as the relative elongation of the representational array along the $V_S$ axis. The aspect ratio increased with token holdings in ACC and showed a weaker tendency in DLPFC and OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}e and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}). Token-holding-label shuffling attenuated this state-related structure, whereas value-label shuffling collapsed the underlying value-space geometry (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}). Together, these results suggest that token holdings modulate offer-value representations across ACC, DLPFC and OFC.

\subsection{Decision-related value representations in prefrontal cortex}
The token-holding-dependent offer-value representations in prefrontal cortex point toward how the earning--spending decision may be formed. A natural decision variable is the value difference, $\Delta V=V_E-V_S$, but we did not observe its robust encoding at the single-unit level. A neuron encoding $\Delta V$ would have opposite tuning directions for $V_E$ and $V_S$. When we grouped neurons by their tuning directions of the two option values, we did not find more neurons had opposite tuning directions (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}a).

An alternative is that value representations become linked to the choice, as suggested by the chosen-value neurons described in OFC \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b}. In the example neuron, earning- and spending-value tuning had opposite signs within each choice condition, and both tuning directions reversed when the selected option changed (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}a,b). This pattern was consistent with encoding the value advantage of the selected option over its alternative. In the pooled populations, $V_E$ encoding was stronger on earning than on spending trials (ACC, $p=3.69\times10^{-5}$; DLPFC, $p=4.47\times10^{-12}$; OFC, $p=3.90\times10^{-18}$) and $V_S$ encoding was stronger on spending than on earning trials (ACC, $p=7.33\times10^{-18}$; DLPFC, $p=4.01\times10^{-2}$; OFC, $p=3.90\times10^{-18}$; two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests across 100 resampling iterations; Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}c and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_conditioned}). Neither choice-label nor value-label shuffling reproduced these differences in the observed direction.

To capture this relationship between value and choice, we defined a relative chosen-value measure, $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}=\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}\cdot(\Delta V-b)$, where $b$ is the fitted choice bias and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ denotes earning ($+1$) or spending ($-1$) choice. This measure expresses the advantage of the selected option relative to the alternative and can be negative. $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ was encoded at both the single-unit (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}b) and population levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}d--f). The pooled population encoding strength followed $V_S>V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}>V_E$ in all three regions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_area}). The strengths of these representations also differed between regions. OFC represented all three value signals robustly, whereas ACC showed little $V_E$ encoding during offer evaluation and DLPFC showed comparatively weak value encoding overall (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}d--f; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_variable}).

Finally, we examined the temporal order of the two offer values, relative chosen value and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ signals. For signals with stable latency estimates, half-peak and onset estimates were overall consistent with the temporal progression from $V_E/V_S$ to $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ to $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}g--i and Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_value_latency} and \ref{tab:supp_value_onset}). This overall pattern is consistent with a transformation from offer-value representations toward choice, with $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ as a potential intermediate representation.

\subsection{Regional organization of good-based and action-based choice signals}
Finally, choice in this task is eventually expressed as eye movements toward the left or right targets. Accordingly, we asked how the choice signals identified above were formed along the good-based and action-based coordinates. The example DLPFC unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}a primarily distinguished leftward from rightward responses, whereas the example OFC unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}b primarily distinguished earning from spending choices.

We used TDR again to separate the two choice coordinates at the population level. Following offer onset, the four choice conditions separated most clearly along the earning/spending axis in OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}c, right) and along the left/right axis in DLPFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}c, middle). The corresponding encoding trajectories and strength comparisons confirmed distinct regional biases in choice format: DLPFC preferentially encoded the left/right action, OFC preferentially encoded the earning/spending identity of the selected option, and ACC carried substantially weaker choice signals overall (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}c and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_strength}). Thus, the relative prominence of the choice representations differed by region and by choice coordinates.

We further asked whether the coordinate encoded more strongly in each region also emerged earlier. No consistent temporal advantage was evident: the relative timing of earning/spending and left/right signals varied across regions and animals and differed between onset and half-peak estimates (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50}). Comparisons of each choice signal across regions likewise identified no brain area in which either signal consistently emerged first (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset_regions} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50_regions}). Regional preference in encoding strength was therefore not accompanied by a corresponding temporal priority.

Together, good-based and action-based choice signals were represented across the OFC and the DLPFC, with OFC preferentially encoding the earning/spending identity of the selected option and DLPFC the left/right action, but without a consistent temporal ordering across signals or regions.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec3}

Economic decisions depend not only on the opportunities currently available but also on the resources already held. In humans, accumulated money constitutes an externally maintained resource state that preserves the capacity for future exchange and influences whether additional resources should be acquired or existing resources consumed. Here, rhesus monkeys earned virtual tokens, retained them across trials and spent them for water. As holdings increased, monkeys shifted systematically from earning toward spending, consistent with a state-dependent utility model in which the relative value of further acquisition declined while the relative value of consumption increased. Neural populations in ACC, OFC and DLPFC represented current holdings, and ACC and OFC updated these representations following resource gains and expenditures. Moreover, resource state modulated option-value coding and reshaped its population geometry across all three regions. Thus, accumulated tokens were represented not merely as previous outcomes, but as a persistent decision context linking past choices to the valuation of future opportunities.

A central contribution of this study is the characterization of an external resource state. Previous studies of state-dependent valuation have focused largely on internal physiological states, such as hunger, thirst and satiety \citep{piech2009appetite,dearaujo2003water,murray2018specializations}. These states alter the current desirability of primary rewards; for example, selective satiety reduces both behavioral preference and OFC responses to the consumed reward \citep{rolls1989hunger,pastorbernier2021satiety}. Token holdings differ in several important respects. They were external to the body, discretely quantifiable and able to increase or decrease without a corresponding change in immediate primary-reward consumption. Whereas satiety reflects the organism’s current physiological relation to a reward, token holdings reflect the externally stored resources available for obtaining that reward.

Internal and external states nevertheless pose a common computational problem. Both must be integrated with current opportunities to determine context-appropriate values. Hunger can increase the value of acquiring food, whereas abundant external resources can reduce the marginal value of further acquisition and increase the attractiveness of consumption. Our results therefore extend the principle of state-dependent valuation from internal motivational conditions to an acquired, externally maintained resource state. Importantly, these states can change independently or even in opposite directions. Earning increased token holdings without producing immediate water, whereas spending decreased holdings while delivering water and potentially increasing satiety.

The present findings build on, but are distinct from, earlier studies of token accumulation and reward saving \citep{seo2009behavioral,yangPrimateAnteriorInsular2022c,ferroAccumulationVirtualTokens2025a,nguyenNeuralRepresentationDecisional2025a,hernadi2015planning}. In many token tasks, however, holdings indicate progress toward a fixed threshold that triggers a reset and automatic delivery of a primary reward \citep{seo2009behavioral,ferroAccumulationVirtualTokens2025a}. Here, tokens instead functioned as spendable secondary resources: animals could voluntarily acquire, preserve or exchange them at intermediate levels. Holdings represented a continuously changing set of future possibilities rather than simply progress toward a predetermined outcome. The principal advance is consequently not only the observation that neurons encode token number, but also the identification of distributed mechanisms that represent and update spendable holdings and use them to modulate acquisition and consumption decisions.

Current holdings were represented across ACC, OFC and DLPFC, paralleling the distributed representation of internal physiological states such as hunger, thirst and satiety \citep{dearaujo2006ensemble,allen2019thirst,encarnacionrivera2025drives}. Individual neurons preferred different levels of holdings, collectively spanning the resource-state space. Such diversity in preferred levels could support a population representation that distinguishes intermediate resource levels and accommodates nonlinear relationships between holdings and behavior. Its distributed nature further suggests that resource information is available to multiple stages of decision making, including state monitoring, valuation and action selection.

Nevertheless, the three regions did not appear to use this information identically. In particular, ACC and OFC showed bidirectional updating following changes in holdings: earning shifted population activity toward states associated with higher holdings, whereas spending shifted it toward lower holdings. This result is important because it suggests that neural activity tracked the current balance of the resource account rather than merely cumulative reward history, task progression or elapsed time. Such nonspecific signals would not necessarily reverse following expenditure. DLPFC represented current holdings but did not show equally robust bidirectional updating, suggesting that representing a resource state and updating it may be partly separable operations. ACC and OFC may contribute more directly to revising the resource account after outcomes, whereas DLPFC may read out the updated state during subsequent choice and action preparation. Because recordings were not simultaneous, however, the present data cannot establish where updating originates or how resource information flows between regions.

Resource state was not simply represented in parallel with option values. It changed how those values were encoded. State-dependent value variables explained neural activity beyond models containing state-independent offer attributes, categorical holdings and choice. At the population level, increasing holdings compressed the neural separation among earning values relative to that among spending values, reshaping value geometry across all three regions. This result distinguishes representing resource state from using resource state. If holdings and option values were encoded independently, changes in holdings might translate population activity while preserving relationships among option values. Instead, holdings altered those relationships, indicating that resource state modulated the relative neural scaling of acquisition and consumption opportunities.

This mechanism provides a potential parallel with internal-state-dependent valuation. Satiety changes neural sensitivity to food-related outcomes \citep{pastorbernier2021satiety,howard2017devaluation}; similarly, token holdings changed neural sensitivity to earning and spending opportunities. In both cases, state may reconfigure value representations so that neural distances reflect the behavioral relevance of options under current conditions. The critical difference lies in the origin of the modulating signal: satiety is an internal physiological state, whereas holdings are acquired external resources whose value derives from future exchange. Our findings therefore suggest that prefrontal circuits can apply state-dependent valuation principles to both internal needs and externally maintained economic resources.

Together, the findings support a recurrent account of economic choice: current holdings provide a resource state; this state modulates earning and spending values; valuation guides choice; and the outcome updates the resource state for the next decision. Resource state is thus both an outcome of previous behavior and an input into future valuation. This closed loop captures a defining feature of economic behavior that is absent from tasks in which decisions are independent and reward delivery terminates the relevant sequence.

The value of an earning or spending opportunity depends on the resources already available. Our task made this dependence observable as monkeys accumulated tokens, spent them for water and retained unspent holdings across trials. Prefrontal activity represented current holdings, tracked their changes after outcomes and incorporated resource state into value-related responses. Here, spending could stop before the wallet was exhausted, allowing animals to return to earning at intermediate holdings. The absence of a fixed exchange threshold also reduced the coupling between holdings and proximity to an externally prescribed redemption goal. Crossing earning rates with spending reward magnitudes then exposed the joint representation of two distinct opportunities across the resource states generated by the animal's own choices. The contribution is therefore not simply that holdings affect preferences, but that resource acquisition and partial consumption can be examined within a shared, continuously changing decision context.

Classic OFC recordings identified subjective offer-value representations \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b}; our results show how such representations relate to currently available resources. The incremental earning-value component and population geometry provided complementary evidence for this dependence around wallet update.
 The measurements do not by themselves establish where or how resource and offer information are combined. In particular, the behavioral model supplies a computational account and predictions for neural analysis rather than a uniquely established valuation mechanism. The precise functional form through which resource state affects value remains a question for future systematic investigation.

At the single-unit level, opposite tuning directions for earning and spending values were not enriched as expected for a dominant value-difference representation. Instead, value tuning depended on the selected option, motivating the relative chosen-value measure $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$. Classic chosen-value studies defined the value of the selected reward on a common scale and evaluated it against alternative candidate variables \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b}. Our measure differs: it is the bias-adjusted advantage of the selected option over its alternative. This definition retains the relative information relevant to the earning--spending decision while making its dependence on choice explicit. Its temporal development between detectable option-value and categorical choice signals suggests a potential intermediate representation, without establishing an independent computational stage.

Such a representation could participate in the evolving competition between options rather than require a separate operation after a choice has already been made. Circuit accounts of OFC similarly allow choice-related activity to develop during competition \citep{ballesta2019inhibition}. Selective attention offers another possible contribution, because OFC value responses vary with fixation location \citep{mcginty2016fixation}. These interpretations distinguish the observed temporal ordering from a claim that each signal marks a discrete processing stage. The present data establish the single-unit encoding patterns and their population timing; they leave open how the relative chosen-value signal contributes to resolving the decision.

The regional distribution further separates resource context from the representation of particular options. ACC strongly represented current holdings and their updates, but showed little earning-value encoding during offer evaluation and comparatively weak choice signals. Its state-dependent value-space geometry was measured around wallet update, so it concerns a different task stage from the limited offer-onset earning signal. OFC combined resource representation and updating with both option values, relative chosen value and good-based choice. This combination connects valuation to the broader proposal that OFC represents task state \citep{schuck2016state}. Comparisons with resource signals in instructed token tasks also depend on the population measure: Nguyen and colleagues distinguished distributed single-neuron reference signals from homogeneous population coding in ventral-bank ACC \citep{nguyenNeuralRepresentationDecisional2025a}. Decoding resource state from heterogeneous activity addresses a different aspect of its representation. Our results suggest regional differences in emphasis rather than an exclusive assignment of resource processing to one area.

DLPFC emphasized action-based choice, whereas OFC emphasized good-based choice, but neither preference established a consistent temporal priority. Earlier work separating offer presentation from the revelation of response targets demonstrated a good-to-action transformation in lateral prefrontal cortex \citep{cai2014contributions}. When option identity and location are available together, however, action-based value processing can participate in decision formation \citep{lin2021evidence}. Our findings support coexistence of both choice formats without resolving a fixed serial order across regions. Within-trial switching poses a related limitation: switching options requires a saccade, whereas staying requires continued fixation. These events therefore cannot isolate switching-related activity from movement-related activity. A task requiring a renewed fixation and an explicit response on every decision would help separate these contributions.

Several model and measurement boundaries guide further work. The behavioral model assumes constant utility per unit of water and does not explicitly include fixation or effort costs. Incorporating intake-dependent water utility and duration-dependent fixation costs could extend the account. Displayed token count also covaries with resource quantity, so varying token denominations or requiring holdings to be inferred would distinguish visual numerosity from an abstract resource representation. Although eliminating a fixed redemption threshold removes one external reference point, it does not determine whether animals use an internal reference. These questions concern the interpretation and generalization of resource-dependent valuation beyond the current task.

Finally, pseudo-population analyses reveal representational structure but do not resolve trial-by-trial interactions between regions. Simultaneous recordings and temporally targeted perturbations could test whether changing a resource representation alters subsequent valuation and earning--spending choice. OFC stimulation studies demonstrate that perturbing value processing can change economic decisions \citep{ballestaValuesEncodedOrbitofrontal2020a,ballestaOrbitofrontalCortexContributes2022}; extending this approach to resource-dependent choice would help distinguish state estimation from valuation and action selection. The present task provides a basis for these questions by linking the resources an animal retains to the neural representation of its next opportunities.

\section{Methods}\label{sec4}

\subsection{Experimental procedures}

\subsubsection{Animals and ethics}
Three male rhesus monkeys (\textit{Macaca mulatta}; monkeys W, U and T), aged 3--4 years at the start of the experiment, were used. Their body weights during the experiments were 6.3--8.0, 3.5--4.6 and 5.8--6.7~kg, respectively. Daily fluid intake was regulated to maintain task motivation. All procedures followed the protocol approved by the Animal Care Committee of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (CEBSIT-2021004).

\subsubsection{Experimental setup and behavioral task}\label{sec:m_task}
Monkeys sat in a custom primate chair with the head fixed and viewed a 23.6-inch monitor at a distance of approximately 60~cm. Visual stimulus presentation and reward delivery were controlled using MonkeyLogic~2 running in MATLAB. Eye position was sampled at 1,000~Hz using an EyeLink~1000 infrared eye tracker. Water was delivered through a computer-controlled solenoid valve calibrated at approximately 0.2~$\mu$l~ms$^{-1}$.

Monkeys performed an earning--spending task (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}a,b) in which tokens accumulated across trials and could subsequently be spent for water. Current holdings were displayed by a central ``wallet'', a $5\times5$ circle array approximately $4.3^\circ$ wide. Filled circles indicated the number of tokens currently held. The wallet remained visible during the inter-trial interval (ITI), when the option stimuli were absent, and updated immediately after each successful earning or spending event. Holdings were bounded between 0 and 25 tokens. Earning at 25 tokens did not add another token, and spending at 0 tokens did not remove one, although the attempted choice was recorded. Each session began at 0 tokens.

Each trial presented one earning stimulus and one spending stimulus. Each option stimulus was approximately $2^\circ$ in diameter. Immediately before option presentation, the monkey's gaze position relative to the screen center was measured. The two options appeared $6^\circ$ from the screen center, one $90^\circ$ clockwise and the other $90^\circ$ counterclockwise from this gaze direction. Assignment of earning and spending stimuli to the two locations was randomized across trials. The earning rate and spending reward were sampled independently with equal probability, yielding 12 offer conditions. The three earning rates were one token per 250, 200 or 100~ms of continuous fixation (4, 5 or 10 tokens/s). Spending occurred once per 200~ms (5 tokens/s). Each spending event deducted one token and provided the water amount specified by the spending stimulus. Water amounts per token were approximately 0, 4, 7 and 11~$\mu$l for monkeys W and U, and 3, 5, 7 and 14~$\mu$l for monkey T.

Monkeys selected an option by directing gaze to it and maintaining fixation within a $3^\circ$-diameter window centered on the option. Continued fixation generated multiple token updates, and monkeys could switch between the two options before trial end. Trial duration was 1,000~ms plus an exponentially distributed interval with a mean of 2,000~ms, redrawn whenever the total exceeded 5,000~ms; the ITI was approximately 1~s. The reward magnitudes above were fixed throughout all recording sessions.

\subsubsection{Surgery, anatomical localization and neural recordings}\label{sec:m_recording}
Before training, each monkey was implanted with a headpost; after behavior stabilized, a recording chamber (25~mm anterior--posterior $\times$ 20~mm medial--lateral) was implanted over prefrontal cortex (left hemisphere in W and T, right in U), and the bone within it was removed with the dura left intact. Surgeries were performed under aseptic conditions and general anesthesia, induced with ketamine hydrochloride (5--15~mg~kg$^{-1}$, i.m.) and maintained with 1.5--2\% isoflurane, with continuous physiological monitoring.

Recording sites were localized using post-implantation structural MRI (Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma, 3~T) and physiological landmarks encountered during electrode penetrations. ACC recordings corresponded approximately to BA~24, OFC to BA~11 and DLPFC to BA~46 (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_recording_sites}).

Neuronal activity was recorded using 16-channel Plexon S-Probes (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, USA). Adjacent recording contacts were separated by 100~$\mu$m, such that the 16 contacts spanned 1.5~mm along the probe shaft. Probes were advanced to the target depth using an EPS multichannel microdrive (Alpha Omega Engineering, Nof HaGalil, Israel). Signals were acquired using an AlphaLab SnR system (Alpha Omega Engineering) at 44~kHz and band-pass filtered online between 300 and 6,000~Hz. No task-responsiveness criterion was applied during offline unit inclusion. Electrophysiological data were collected over 150 recording sessions (26 in monkey W, 61 in U and 63 in T). After unit inclusion (see \nameref{sec:m_units}), the dataset comprised 715 units from ACC (251 from monkey W and 464 from T; no ACC recordings were obtained from U), 1,113 from DLPFC (110, 481 and 522 from W, U and T) and 962 from OFC (106, 435 and 421 from W, U and T).

\subsection{Data preparation}

\subsubsection{Task events}\label{sec:m_events}
We define \emph{offer onset} as the simultaneous appearance of the two option stimuli, an \emph{initial choice} as the first option fixation that successfully produced an earning or spending event, and a \emph{wallet update} as an individual token addition or deduction. Within a period of continuous fixation, a \emph{choice} is the interval between two consecutive wallet updates. The wallet update ending choice $k$ is its \emph{choice outcome} and simultaneously the \emph{choice onset} of choice $k+1$. A \emph{choice switch} is a transition to the other option in which both the preceding and subsequent fixations produced a token event and the inter-fixation interval was nonnegative and shorter than 150~ms.

\subsubsection{Event inclusion}\label{sec:m_inclusion}
All behavioral sessions in which electrophysiological data were recorded were included. Behavioral analyses used only the first choice on each trial, provided that it was successfully executed, thereby avoiding dependence introduced by repeated within-trial selections. Observations at the wallet boundaries (0 or 25 tokens) were also excluded because one option could not change the token holdings. Consequently, all behavioral value models were evaluated over holdings of 1--24 tokens.

Neural analyses aligned to within-fixation wallet updates included only intermediate choices, excluding the first and last choice of each continuous fixation, so that no saccade occurred at the aligned wallet updates.

\subsubsection{Task variables and notation}\label{sec:m_variables}
Token holdings, denoted by $H$, are the number of tokens displayed in the wallet at the alignment event specified for each analysis. Choice-onset analyses assigned each event the earning/spending choice beginning at that update and the post-update token holdings; choice-outcome analyses assigned the choice ending at that update and the pre-update token holdings. Value predictors were computed from the offer information and token holdings assigned in this way. In neural models, $H$ entered as a continuous predictor (1--24 tokens), as a categorical predictor with five token-holding groups, or as a categorical predictor with one level per token count (up to 24 levels), as stated for each model. The five token-holding groups were [1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15] and [16--24] tokens. These groups were approximately logarithmic, yielding more comparable trial counts across groups. Geometry analyses used three approximately logarithmic groups---1, 2--5 and 6--24 tokens---to maintain sufficient events per offer-condition cell.

$V_E$ and $V_S$ denote earning and spending values from the free-$\alpha$ utility model; $V_E^{\mathrm{obj}}$ and $V_S^{\mathrm{obj}}$ denote the state-independent predictors from the fixed-$\alpha$ model (see \nameref{sec:m_param}). $\Delta V=V_E-V_S$ denotes the unnormalized value difference. $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ denotes earning ($+1$) versus spending ($-1$), and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{LR}}$ denotes left ($+1$) versus right ($-1$) choices, defined by whether the chosen option appeared to the left or right of the screen center; the two options were always placed on opposite sides of the center (see \nameref{sec:m_task}). Unless otherwise stated, ``choice'' refers to the earning/spending choice; $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{LR}}$ is used only in the analyses of good-based and action-based choice signals (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}). $V_L$ and $V_R$ are obtained by assigning $V_E$ and $V_S$ to the corresponding option locations. $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}=\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}\cdot(\Delta V-b)$ denotes the relative chosen-value measure, where $b$ is the choice bias of the parametric utility model.

\subsubsection{Spike sorting and unit inclusion}\label{sec:m_units}
Spike sorting was performed automatically using Kilosort4 \cite{pachitariu2024kilosort4}, with each recording fragment sorted independently; units classified as good or multi-unit activity (MUA) were retained. To exclude silent periods caused by electrode drift, loss of isolation or cell injury, firing rate was evaluated in complete non-overlapping 60-s windows. For each unit, only the longest continuous sequence of windows with a mean firing rate strictly above 1~Hz was retained. Trials were required to lie completely within this active interval. At least 500 trials were required per unit.

\subsubsection{Firing-rate estimation}
Spike trains were initially converted to firing rates in 10-ms bins. Analyses based on a single mean firing rate within a predefined window used unsmoothed rates unless otherwise stated. PSTHs and other time-resolved trajectories were smoothed with a causal Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 100$~ms). The kernel spanned only $[-300,0]$~ms relative to each time point, so the smoothed rate depended on activity in the current and preceding bins.

\subsection{Behavioral value models and validation}

\subsubsection{Non-parametric choice model}\label{sec:m_nonparam}
To quantify the modulation of option value by token holdings (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}d) without assuming a specific functional form, we estimated subjective weights for the seven option stimuli (three earning, four spending) at each of the 24 token-holding levels. Earning probability was modeled by a sigmoid function:
\begin{equation}
P_\mathrm{Earn} = \sigma\bigl(V_e^{\mathrm{np}}(H) - V_s^{\mathrm{np}}(H)\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma(x)=1/(1+\exp(-x))$, $V_j^{\mathrm{np}}(H)$ is the estimated weight of option stimulus $j$ ($j=1,\ldots,7$) at token holding $H$, and $e$ and $s$ index the earning and spending stimuli offered on that trial. To regularize the solution and encourage gradual variation across adjacent levels, we augmented the cross-entropy loss with an $L_2$ smoothing penalty:
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
L = {}& -\sum_{i} \bigl[c_i \ln \hat{p}_i + (1 - c_i) \ln(1 - \hat{p}_i)\bigr] \\
& + \lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}} \sum_{j=1}^{7}\sum_{H=1}^{23} \bigl(V_j^{\mathrm{np}}(H) - V_j^{\mathrm{np}}(H+1)\bigr)^2,
\end{split}
\end{equation}
where the sum runs over trials $i$, the included trials of a monkey pooled across its sessions, $c_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary choice (1 = earn, 0 = spend), $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted earning probability, and $\lambda_{\mathrm{smooth}} = 10$ controls the smoothing strength.

The model contained no separate bias term or fixed reference option. Choice probabilities depend on differences between option weights; a common constant offset leaves both the likelihood and the smoothing penalty unchanged. Interpretation therefore concerns relative weights and their state dependence rather than an absolute value baseline.

\subsubsection{Parametric utility model}\label{sec:m_param}
We developed a parametric model based on marginal utility theory (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}e and Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}a,b). We assumed a power-law utility function $U(H) = H^\alpha$, where $\alpha$ is the curvature exponent, with marginal utility $U'(H)=\alpha H^{\alpha-1}$. Reward magnitude was expressed in dimensionless units by dividing the water amounts specified under \nameref{sec:m_task} by the second-smallest amount within each monkey. The subjective values of earning and spending were defined as:
\begin{align}
V_E &= \frac{\mathit{ES}}{\mathit{ER}}\,U'(H), \label{eq:VE}\\
V_S &= R \cdot \mathit{SS} - \frac{\mathit{SS}}{\mathit{ER}}\,U'(H), \label{eq:VS}
\end{align}
where $\mathit{ES}$ is the earning speed (tokens/s), $R$ is the normalized reward magnitude (in arbitrary units), $\mathit{ER}$ is the exchange rate (tokens per unit reward), and $\mathit{SS} = 5$ tokens/s is the spend speed. The first term in $V_S$ represents the immediate reward benefit; the second captures the opportunity cost of spending a token scaled by its marginal utility. Choice probability was given by:
\begin{equation}
P_\mathrm{Earn} = \sigma\bigl((\Delta V-b)/\gamma\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $\gamma > 0$ is a temperature parameter (smaller values imply greater sensitivity) and $b$ is a choice bias. Free parameters $(\alpha,\, \mathit{ER},\, b,\, \gamma)$ were estimated by minimizing the cross-entropy loss, with $\alpha\geq0$, $\mathit{ER}>0$ and $\gamma>0$; $b$ was unconstrained.

The \textit{fixed-$\alpha$} model served as a nested baseline in which $\alpha$ was constrained to 1, corresponding to linear utility and constant marginal utility. All remaining parameters were estimated freely, testing whether state dependence improved prediction within this model family.

\subsubsection{Model validation and session-wise fitting}\label{sec:m_validation}
To evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}a), we applied leave-one-session-out (LOSO) cross-validation separately within each subject. In each fold, model parameters were estimated from the pooled data from all remaining sessions and evaluated on the held-out session. Performance on the held-out session was quantified as the average negative log-likelihood per trial (NLL per trial). The per-fold loss difference was defined as $\Delta L=L_\mathrm{fixed}-L_\mathrm{free}$, such that positive values indicate better generalization of the free-$\alpha$ model. Loss differences were tested against zero across held-out sessions using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

To characterize the distribution of fitted utility curvature across recording sessions (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}b), the free-$\alpha$ model was fitted independently to each session.

\subsubsection{Reaction-time analysis}\label{sec:m_rt}
Reaction time (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}d) was defined as the interval from offer onset to target acquisition (gaze entry into the fixation window of the initially chosen option). Analyses included all recording sessions and used valid initial choices at holdings of 1--24 tokens. Decision difficulty was indexed by the absolute normalized value difference, $|\widetilde{\Delta V}|=|(\Delta V-b)/\gamma|$, with smaller values indicating proximity to the fitted behavioral indifference point. Within each subject, trials were divided at the median into hard (at or below the median) and easy (above the median) groups. After grouping, reaction times exceeding 700~ms were excluded. Reaction-time distributions were compared within each subject using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests on pooled trials.

\subsection{Targeted dimensionality reduction}\label{sec:m_tdr_all}

\subsubsection{TDR rationale}\label{sec:m_tdr_principle}
Individual prefrontal neurons can encode mixtures of correlated task variables. Following Mante et al.~\cite{mante2013context}, we used targeted dimensionality reduction (TDR) to estimate population axes that separately account for response variance associated with each variable. To avoid overestimating encoding through overfitting, axes were estimated on training events and evaluated on held-out events. Each of 100 independent resampling iterations comprised five steps: (i) split: each unit's events were divided into training and held-out sets; (ii) regression: each unit's training responses were regressed on the task variables; (iii) orthogonalization: coefficient vectors were stacked across units and orthogonalized by QR decomposition; (iv) readout: held-out responses were combined into pseudo-population trials and projected onto the resulting axes; and (v) quantification: encoding strength was computed from these projections. Analysis-specific response windows, predictors and their QR order, groups and group labels are listed in Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}.

\subsubsection{Axis estimation and held-out readout}\label{sec:m_tdr}\label{sec:m_sampling}
\textbf{(i) Split.} Each unit's events were split equally at random into training and held-out sets. Units were eligible if they had at least 50 valid events and at least five held-out events in each group (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}).

\textbf{(ii) Regression.} Responses $\mathbf y_n(t)$ were z-scored at each time point using training means and standard deviations, giving $\mathbf z_n(t)$; the same transformation was applied to held-out responses. In all TDR models, $H$ entered as a continuous predictor (1--24 tokens). Predictors were z-scored using training events so that coefficients were comparable across predictors. For unit $n$,
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol\beta_n=(\mathbf F_n^\top\mathbf F_n)^{-1}\mathbf F_n^\top\bar{\mathbf z}_n,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf F_n$ is the standardized training predictor matrix and $\bar{\mathbf z}_n$ is $\mathbf z_n(t)$ of training events averaged over the fitting window. The choice-format analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}c) used ridge regression, adding $\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}\mathbf I$ to $\mathbf F_n^\top\mathbf F_n$.

\textbf{(iii) Orthogonalization.} Eligible units were sampled without replacement, up to 300 for pooled populations and 100 for individual monkeys. Their coefficient vectors were stacked and orthogonalized by QR decomposition:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf B=[\boldsymbol\beta_1,\ldots,\boldsymbol\beta_N]^\top,
\qquad \mathbf B_\pi=\mathbf Q\mathbf R,
\end{equation}
where $\pi$ is the analysis-specific column order. The axis $\mathbf q_v$ is the column of $\mathbf Q$ corresponding to variable $v$, that is, the normalized component of the coefficient column $\boldsymbol\beta_v$ orthogonal to the columns preceding it in $\pi$. Column orders and extracted axes are listed in Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}.

\textbf{(iv) Readout.} For held-out group $g$, pseudo-trial $m$ combined one held-out event per unit, drawn with replacement from that unit's events in $g$:
\begin{equation}
\widetilde{\mathbf x}_{g,m}(t)=[z_{1,i_1}(t),\ldots,z_{N,i_N}(t)]^\top,
\qquad s_{g,m}(t)=\mathbf q_v^\top\widetilde{\mathbf x}_{g,m}(t),
\end{equation}
where $z_{n,i}(t)$ is the z-scored held-out response of unit $n$ on event $i$.

\textbf{(v) Quantification.} Encoding strength was quantified as
\begin{equation}
r^2(t)=\mathrm{corr}\bigl(\mathbf s(t),\boldsymbol\ell\bigr)^2,
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf s(t)$ contains the pseudo-trial projections and $\boldsymbol\ell$ their group labels.

\textbf{Exceptions and controls.} Updating (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}b,c; \S\ref{sec:m_updating}) and geometry (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}d,e; \S\ref{sec:m_geometry}) instead used one mean projection per group. Shuffle controls permuted labels within units and repeated steps (i)--(v).

\subsubsection{Latency}\label{sec:m_latency}
Latency was measured from each $r^2(t)$ trajectory using the pre-offer baseline $[-200,0)$~ms. Let $\theta$ be the 95th percentile of $r^2(t)$ within the baseline. Onset latency was
\begin{equation}
t_{\mathrm{onset}}=\min\{t_k\geq0:r^2(t_{k+j})>\theta\ \text{for }j=0,\ldots,4\},
\end{equation}
where $t_k$ are consecutive time bins. Half-peak time was
\begin{equation}
t_{50}=\min\left\{t\in[0,T_{\mathrm{peak}}]:r^2(t)\geq\frac{\mathrm{median}(r^2_{\mathrm{baseline}})+r^2(T_{\mathrm{peak}})}{2}\right\}.
\end{equation}
An iteration yielded no $t_{50}$ if its peak did not exceed $\theta$, and no onset latency if no five consecutive bins exceeded $\theta$; each signal's valid estimates were retained separately.

\subsection{Token-holding representation and updating}

\subsubsection{Single-unit token-holding selectivity and tuning}\label{sec:m_tuning}
To quantify token-holding selectivity (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}e--k and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}), we used OLS regression to model the mean firing rate over $[-300,300)$~ms relative to offer onset against task variables:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 \mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}} + \beta_4 H
    \label{eq:cat_wealth}
\end{equation}
where $H$ was the five-level categorical token-holding predictor at offer onset defined under \nameref{sec:m_variables}. Units were classified as token-holding selective if the $F$-test for the $H$ term reached significance ($p<0.05$). The preferred token-holding level was defined as the level yielding the smallest $p$-value across two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, each comparing the mean firing rate of one token-holding group against all remaining groups combined. The direction of tuning was determined by the sign of $\overline{\mathrm{FR}}_{\mathrm{pref}} - \overline{\mathrm{FR}}_{\mathrm{rest}}$. For population averaging, the tuning curves of negatively tuned units ($\overline{\mathrm{FR}}_{\mathrm{pref}} - \overline{\mathrm{FR}}_{\mathrm{rest}} < 0$) were inverted prior to normalization. Min-max normalization was then applied:
\begin{equation}
    \mathit{TC}_{\mathrm{norm}} = \frac{\mathit{TC} - \min(\mathit{TC})}{\max(\mathit{TC}) - \min(\mathit{TC})}
\end{equation}
Normalized curves were averaged across units sharing the same preferred token-holding level to yield group-level population tuning curves. To characterize the shape of the re-centered population tuning curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}i--k), the normalized firing rates at relative positions $\pm1$ and $\pm2$ from each unit's preferred token-holding level were extracted and compared using a two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test.

For the shuffle control, token-holding-group labels were independently permuted across trials within each unit before repeating unit selection, preferred-group assignment, sign alignment, normalization and peak re-centering. Selective proportions (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}e and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}a) were averaged across ten permutations per unit, whereas the population tuning curves and their associated statistical comparisons (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}b) used the first permutation. Individual-monkey analyses repeated the complete procedure used for the pooled results.

\subsubsection{Cross-temporal decoding}\label{sec:m_cross}
For cross-temporal decoding (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}a and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_cross_temporal}), at each 50-ms training bin, a token-holding axis was fitted using $V_E$, $V_S$, $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ and continuous $H$ (1--24 tokens) and applied to held-out responses at all bins in both epochs, yielding a $24\times24$ matrix. Encoding strength was averaged across iterations.

For the shuffle control, token-holding labels ($H$) were permuted across events within each unit, separately for each epoch; $V_E$, $V_S$ and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ were left unchanged. Each matrix entry was compared with the shuffle distribution using a one-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum test; entries not exceeding shuffle at $p<0.05$ were displayed in black.

\subsubsection{Choice-dependent updating of token-holding representations}\label{sec:m_updating}
For choice-dependent updating (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}b,c and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_state_updating}), TDR models with $V_E$, $V_S$, $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ and continuous $H$, in this QR order, were fitted to responses averaged over $[-50,150)$~ms relative to choice outcome. Held-out projections onto the $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ and $H$ axes were obtained for the five token-holding groups crossed with earning and spending (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}). Axes were oriented toward earning and higher token holdings using pre-update group means. Within each iteration, each token-group trajectory was referenced to its first sample and regressed on time; the temporal coefficient, defined as the mean of the five group slopes, was computed separately for earning and spending updates; positive and negative coefficients indicated movement toward higher and lower represented holdings, respectively. The displayed trajectories used an independent split and all eligible units.

Temporal coefficients were tested against zero using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests. Bidirectional updating required a significantly positive earning coefficient and a significantly negative spending coefficient (both $p<0.05$). Choice-label and token-holding-label shuffles permuted $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ or $H$, respectively, across events within each unit, with the other predictors unchanged, and repeated regression, QR and projection; pooled analyses included both controls, and real-data analyses were repeated for individual monkeys.

\subsection{Token-holding-dependent offer-value encoding}

\subsubsection{Nested-model comparison}\label{sec:m_nested}
For nested-model comparisons (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}c and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}c), 10-ms firing rates were smoothed with the causal Gaussian kernel described above and averaged in ten non-overlapping 100-ms windows spanning $-500$ to 500~ms relative to choice onset. Units required at least 100 valid events spanning all five token-holding groups.

Two nested linear models were fitted in each window. The baseline model ($M_1$) included state-independent offer values, choice and token holdings:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_2 V_S^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3 \mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}} + \beta_4 H,
\end{equation}
where $V_E^{\mathrm{obj}}$ and $V_S^{\mathrm{obj}}$ are token-holding-independent values estimated from equations~\eqref{eq:VE} and~\eqref{eq:VS} with $\alpha=1$. $H$ entered as a categorical predictor with one level per token count (1--24), so that any effect of holdings alone, however nonlinear, was absorbed by the baseline. The token-holding-modulated model ($M_2$) added the free-$\alpha$ earning value:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_2 V_S^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3 \mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}} + \beta_4 H + \beta_5 V_E.
\end{equation}
Because $V_E$ is a product of an offer term and a holdings term (equation~\eqref{eq:VE}), it is not spanned by $M_1$. An analogous increment could not be estimated for $V_S$: as shown in equation~\eqref{eq:VS}, $V_S$ is an additive combination of an offer term and a holdings term, both already spanned by $V_S^{\mathrm{obj}}$ and the categorical $H$ in $M_1$.
Model performance was quantified by adjusted $R^2$ to penalize the additional parameter in $M_2$. The modulation index $\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}(M_2)-R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}(M_1)$ was computed for every unit and time window. For the shuffled-$V_E$ control, the added token-holding-dependent earning-value predictor was permuted once across all valid events within each unit; firing rates and baseline predictors were unchanged. Observed and shuffled per-unit increments were compared using a paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test in each 100-ms time bin.

\subsubsection{Population value-space geometry}\label{sec:m_geometry}
We used TDR-derived $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes to visualize the joint population representation of earning and spending offers around choice onset (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}d,e and Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}). The task contained 12 offer conditions formed by crossing three earning rates with four spending reward magnitudes. The lowest spending-reward option was rarely chosen and therefore provided too few observations for stable condition averages. We excluded this condition from the geometry analysis, leaving the $3\times3$ subset formed by the three earning rates and the three retained spending-reward levels. The three token-holding groups were defined under \nameref{sec:m_variables}. For each unit, z-scored held-out responses were averaged across all events within each of the 27 conditions (nine offer conditions $\times$ three token-holding groups), yielding one condition mean per unit; these unit-wise means were stacked across units and projected onto the axes to obtain 27 population states. The TDR model included $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$, continuous $H$, $V_E$ and $V_S$, in this QR order, fitted to responses averaged over $[0,150)$~ms after choice onset; the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes were extracted (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}).

To summarize the scale of the projected geometry along each value axis, the nine coordinates on each axis were sorted within every token-holding group and resampling iteration. We defined the trimmed earning-axis and spending-axis ranges as
\begin{align}
D_E &= s^E_{(8)}-s^E_{(2)},\\
D_S &= s^S_{(8)}-s^S_{(2)},
\end{align}
where $s^E_{(k)}$ and $s^S_{(k)}$ denote the $k$th ordered projections on the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes, respectively. Excluding the single most extreme coordinate at each end reduced the influence of any one condition mean. Within each token-holding group and resampling iteration, the aspect ratio was defined as
\begin{equation}
A=\frac{D_S}{D_E}.
\end{equation}
An aspect ratio greater than 1 indicates that the offer representation extends further along the spending-value axis than along the earning-value axis. Aspect ratios and component ranges were summarized as mean $\pm$ SD across all 100 resampling iterations. For display (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}d), we considered only iterations in which $D_E$ changed strictly monotonically across the three token-holding groups in the direction predicted by the behavioral model (ACC, 64; DLPFC, 14; OFC, 25 of 100 iterations). Among these, the iteration maximizing the product of the eight squared Pearson correlations between condition-center projections and model values ($V_E$ and $V_S$, within each token-holding group and across all groups) multiplied by the magnitude of the slope of $D_E$ across token-holding groups was shown.

Two shuffle controls were applied to the pooled dataset. Before the training/held-out split, either token counts (token-holding-label shuffle) or offer values ($V_E$ and $V_S$ as pairs; value-label shuffle) were permuted across all eligible events within each unit. The complete procedure was then repeated.

\subsection{Decision-related value representations}

\subsubsection{Single-unit value selectivity}\label{sec:m_unit}
\textbf{Joint encoding of earning and spending values.} To test whether single units preferentially encoded the value difference $\Delta V$ (Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}a), we fit an OLS regression model to mean firing rates over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset and over $[0,150)$~ms after choice onset:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 \mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}} + \beta_4 H,
\end{equation}
where $H$ entered as a categorical predictor with one level per token count observed for the unit (up to 24 levels). Fits required at least 20 valid events per unit in the corresponding epoch. Units with a significant $\beta_1$ ($p<0.05$) were classified as $V_E^{+}$ or $V_E^{-}$ according to its sign, and units with a significant $\beta_2$ as $V_S^{+}$ or $V_S^{-}$. Among units significant for both $V_E$ and $V_S$, we constructed a $2 \times 2$ contingency table (rows: $V_E$ sign; columns: $V_S$ sign) and applied a two-sided Fisher's exact test to assess sign association. We inspected its direction to determine whether the anti-diagonal cells (opposite-sign tuning: $V_E^{+}\times V_S^{-}$ and $V_E^{-}\times V_S^{+}$) were over-represented, as would be expected if $\Delta V$ were the dominant encoded quantity.

\textbf{Relative chosen-value selectivity.} We defined a relative chosen-value measure $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ as
\begin{equation}
    V_{\mathrm{Chosen}} = \mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}\cdot(\Delta V-b),
\end{equation}
where $b$ is the choice bias of the parametric utility model (see \nameref{sec:m_param}).
This variable is the signed value advantage of the chosen option over the unchosen option relative to the fitted indifference point. The regression model was
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 V_{\mathrm{Chosen}} + \beta_4 \mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}} + \beta_5 H,
\end{equation}
with $H$ again a categorical predictor with one level per observed token count, fit to mean unsmoothed firing rates over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset. Fits required at least 20 valid offer-onset events per unit. This model was used for all five encoding proportions in Extended Data Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}b. Selectivity for $V_E$, $V_S$, $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ was assessed using two-sided tests of the corresponding regression coefficients ($p<0.05$). Token-holding selectivity was assessed using a joint partial $F$-test comparing the full model with a reduced model omitting $H$ ($p<0.05$). For these five encoding proportions, empirical false-positive proportions were estimated using ten response permutations within each unit. The response vector was permuted across valid offer-onset events while the complete predictor matrix was held fixed before refitting the same model. Shuffle proportions were averaged across repetitions.

\subsubsection{Choice-conditioned value encoding}\label{sec:m_conditioned}
For choice-conditioned value encoding (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}c and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_conditioned}), earning and spending event pools were downsampled without replacement to equal sizes before training/test splitting. Units were eligible if both choice pools contained at least 50 events after downsampling, with at least five held-out events in each $V_E$ and $V_S$ tertile within each choice. Separately for earning and spending events, TDR models with continuous $H$, $V_E$ and $V_S$, in this QR order, were fitted to responses averaged over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset, and held-out projections onto the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes were grouped by value tertiles within each choice (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}). 

Within each iteration, encoding strength was averaged over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset. We defined $\Delta r^2_{V_E}=r^2(V_E\mid\mathrm{Earn})-r^2(V_E\mid\mathrm{Spend})$ and $\Delta r^2_{V_S}=r^2(V_S\mid\mathrm{Spend})-r^2(V_S\mid\mathrm{Earn})$ and tested each against zero using a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. For the shuffle controls, either $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ labels (choice-label shuffle) or offer values ($V_E$ and $V_S$ as pairs; value-label shuffle) were permuted across all valid events within each unit, and the complete procedure, starting from downsampling, was then repeated. The complete procedure was repeated for individual monkeys.

\subsubsection{Population encoding of value and choice}\label{sec:m_population}
For population encoding of value and choice (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}d--i and Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_area}--\ref{tab:supp_onset_by_variable}), a joint model included continuous $H$ (1--24 tokens), $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$, $V_E$, $V_S$ and $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}).

Encoding strength was averaged over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}d--f). Strengths of $V_E$, $V_S$ and $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ were compared between signals within each region using two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_area}) and between regions using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_variable}). Half-peak and onset latencies (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}g--i) were estimated as defined under \nameref{sec:m_latency}. Within each region, $V_E$, $V_S$ and $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ were each compared with $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$, and $V_E$ and $V_S$ with $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_value_latency} and \ref{tab:supp_value_onset}); the three value signals were also compared between regions (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_latency_by_variable} and \ref{tab:supp_onset_by_variable}). Latency comparisons used two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests on each signal's valid estimates.

\subsection{Good-based and action-based choice representations}\label{sec:m_goodaction}
The population choice-format model (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}c and Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_strength}--\ref{tab:supp_choice_t50_regions}) regressed responses averaged over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset on $V_L$, $V_R$, $V_E$, $V_S$, continuous $H$, $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{LR}}$ and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$, and the $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{LR}}$ and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ axes were extracted (Table~\ref{tab:tdr_parameters}). Because $V_L+V_R=V_E+V_S$ on every event, the value predictors are exactly collinear and the OLS solution is not unique; this model was therefore fit by ridge regression with $\lambda_{\mathrm{ridge}}=1$.

Encoding strength was averaged over $[0,500)$~ms after offer onset. Strengths of the $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{ES}}$ and $\mathit{Choice}_{\mathrm{LR}}$ signals were compared within each region using two-sided paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_strength}). Onset and half-peak latencies, defined under \nameref{sec:m_latency}, were compared between the two signals within each region (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50}) and for each signal between regions (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset_regions} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50_regions}), using two-sided unpaired Wilcoxon rank-sum tests on each signal's valid estimates. Analyses were repeated for pooled and individual-monkey populations.


The two-dimensional display used an independent split and all eligible units; each of the four groups formed by crossing earning/spending with left/right choice required at least five held-out events. Within each unit and joint group, the same 75 replacement-drawn events were projected onto both coordinates. At each time point, the standard deviation of pseudo-trial Euclidean distances from the mean trajectory defined a circle radius; an alpha shape around the combined circle points formed the displayed envelope. This envelope illustrates trajectory dispersion rather than an axis-wise standard deviation or confidence region.
