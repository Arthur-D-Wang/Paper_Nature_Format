\abstract{Animals must balance acquiring resources for future use against consuming current holdings. How the brain incorporates changing holdings into earning--spending decisions remains unclear. Three macaques chose between earning tokens and spending them for water, with holdings carried across trials and spending possible without exhausting the wallet. As holdings increased, monkeys shifted toward spending, a pattern captured by a resource-dependent utility model. Recordings from anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) revealed resource-state representations and state-dependent value signals. ACC and OFC updated resource representations after outcomes. Around wallet updates, resource state modulated offer-value representations and their population geometry. During offer evaluation, relative chosen-value signals developed between option-value and categorical choice signals. OFC carried the most comprehensive value and good-based choice representations, whereas DLPFC emphasized action-based choice. These findings link changing resource holdings to distributed prefrontal representations of value and choice.}

\keywords{Resource state, Earning--spending, Orbitofrontal cortex, Anterior cingulate cortex, Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Animals often face conflicting demands: obtaining more of one resource can come at the expense of satisfying another need \cite{stephens1986foraging,hengeveld2009optimal}. Efficient allocation therefore depends not only on the available outcomes but also on the resources already held. As one resource accumulates, the marginal value of acquiring more may decline relative to alternative outcomes, as described by diminishing marginal utility \cite{bernoulli1954exposition,stephens1986foraging}. In human societies, money makes this problem ubiquitous: because it can be accumulated and exchanged across needs, people repeatedly choose between earning more for future use and spending current holdings for immediate benefit. However, studies in humans alone cannot determine whether this dependence on monetary holdings is a product of human institutions or reflects a more general capacity, nor can they reveal its implementation at the level of individual neurons. Addressing these questions requires an animal model that can accumulate and exchange a symbolic resource while permitting direct measurement of neural activity. Non-human primates (NHPs) meet these requirements: they can acquire, retain and exchange tokens \cite{sousaUseTokensRewards2001,chenHowBasicAre2006a,addessiAreRootsHuman2020,beran2021non,leca2021acquisition,seo2009behavioral}, and their economic preferences change with accumulated token holdings \cite{yangPrimateAnteriorInsular2022c,ferroAccumulationVirtualTokens2025a}. What remains missing is a paradigm that joins two questions previously studied largely apart: how current token holdings affect decisions, and how values are assigned and compared across reward categories. Token gain--loss tasks and tasks with prescribed exchange thresholds address important aspects of resource-dependent behavior, but do not directly resolve how an animal balances continued earning against partial consumption of its holdings. This requires allowing both earning and spending to continue or stop at intermediate resource states while independently varying the two offers.

This behavioral problem defines a neural computation linking current resource state to option value and ultimately to choice. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) have all been implicated in value-based decision-making. ACC activity tracks accumulated token holdings and other wealth-like reference states, making it a candidate for representing and updating resource state \cite{seo2009behavioral,juechemsVentromedialPrefrontalCortex2017,nguyenNeuralRepresentationDecisional2025a}. OFC broadly represents offer values, chosen values and choices between goods \cite{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b,padoa-schioppaOrbitofrontalCortexNeural2017,rudebeckOrbitofrontalCortex2018,ballestaValuesEncodedOrbitofrontal2020a,ballestaOrbitofrontalCortexContributes2022}. DLPFC also carries value-related activity, together with strong spatial and action-related signals \cite{cai2014contributions,lin2021evidence}. These findings characterize the component signals, but not how they operate together when resource state and cross-category valuation must be combined. Specifically, it is unknown how current holdings modulate the values of earning and spending, what additional value computations are represented as the decision develops, and how the resulting decision is expressed in good-based and action-based coordinates.

Here we combined a new earning--spending task with computational modeling and single-neuron recordings from ACC, OFC and DLPFC in three rhesus monkeys. Token holdings carried continuously across trials as monkeys chose between acquiring tokens and spending them for water, thereby coupling a changing resource state to cross-category value comparison. As holdings increased, monkeys shifted systematically from earning to spending, and a state-dependent utility model explained this shift through opposing changes in the relative values of the two options. Across prefrontal cortex, neural populations represented current resource state, incorporated it into option-value coding, and carried option-value, relative chosen-value and categorical choice signals with distinct temporal profiles. ACC and OFC updated resource-state representations after outcomes; OFC carried the most complete option-value and good-based choice signals, whereas DLPFC predominantly represented action-based choice. Together, these results link the representation and updating of resource state to value processing and earning--spending choice.

\subsection{Resource state shifts earning--spending preferences}

Three rhesus macaques were trained on an earning--spending task (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}A) in which they chose between acquiring virtual tokens and spending accumulated tokens for water rewards. Each trial paired one of three earning options, distinguished by earning rate ($V_\text{Earn}$), with one of four spending options, distinguished by water magnitude ($V_\text{Spend}$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}B). Tokens carried over across trials until spent, so accumulated token holdings constituted a continuously evolving resource state. Each choice therefore depended on both the current offers and the resources available at that moment.

The initial choice on each trial revealed two consistent behavioral patterns (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}C). First, earning probability followed the illustrative color ordering of offer conditions: across resource states, monkeys were more likely to earn when the earning rate was faster and the spending reward was smaller. This ordering indicates that all three monkeys distinguished the offer contingencies. Second, within individual offer conditions, earning probability generally declined as tokens accumulated. Thus, choices reflected not only the current offers but also the animal's current resource state.

This state-dependent shift is consistent with diminishing marginal utility: accumulating tokens reduces the marginal benefit of earning an additional token and the opportunity cost of spending one. To assess the change in valuation without imposing a particular functional form, we estimated non-parametric subjective weights for the seven option stimuli at each of the 24 token levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}D). In all three monkeys, the subjective weights of earning options decreased, whereas those of spending options increased, as tokens accumulated, showing a state-dependent shift in the relative weights favoring spending over earning.

We next formalized this account using a parametric choice model based on diminishing marginal utility (see Methods). The model integrated current resource state with the offer attributes to estimate earning and spending values. Earning probability was a sigmoid function of the bias-adjusted, scaled value difference, $(\Delta V-b)/\gamma$, where $\Delta V=V_E-V_S$, in all three monkeys (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}E and Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}C). This account generalized to held-out sessions and was consistently recovered in session-wise fits across all three subjects (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}A,B). Moreover, the resulting decision variable predicted decision time: responses were slower near the fitted behavioral indifference point (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}D). Together, these results support a stable resource-state-dependent account of choice and relate the bias-adjusted value difference to both choice and decision time.

\subsection{Distributed prefrontal tuning represents current resource state}
Having established that current resource state systematically shifted earning--spending preferences, we next asked how it was represented by individual prefrontal neurons. We divided holdings into five approximately logarithmically spaced groups ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15] and [16--24] tokens) to accommodate the compressed scaling commonly observed for numerical magnitude \cite{niederMiller2003compressed,niederDehaene2009number}, while yielding more comparable trial counts across groups. An ACC neuron responded most strongly in the highest resource-state group (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}A,B), whereas an OFC neuron showed maximal firing in the intermediate [8--15]-token group (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}C,D). Across ACC, DLPFC and OFC, preferred resource states were distributed across all five groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}E).

We next asked how these single-neuron tuning functions were organized at the population level. Neurons preferring different resource-state groups exhibited similarly shaped tuning curves centered at different points along the resource-state axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}F--H). Re-centering the curves on their preferred group revealed a common profile in all three regions, with firing rates declining progressively as resource state moved away from the preferred level (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}I--K). This graded decline disappeared when resource-state-group labels were shuffled and the complete analysis was repeated, indicating that it did not arise solely from selecting and aligning response peaks (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}). Together, these heterogeneous tuning profiles provided a distributed population representation capable of distinguishing different levels of the animal's current resource state.

\subsection{Temporal dynamics and choice-dependent updating of resource-state representations}
The preceding analyses established that prefrontal populations represented current resource state, but did not reveal how this representation evolved across task epochs. We therefore applied cross-temporal decoding based on targeted dimensionality reduction (TDR) \cite{mante2013context}: at each time bin, a TDR-derived resource-state axis was fitted independently and tested at every other bin, yielding a $24 \times 24$ matrix of decoding accuracy ($R^2$) spanning the offer-onset and wallet-update epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}A). Resource state was decodable even before offer onset in all three regions, consistent with the wallet remaining visible throughout the ITI. During the offer-onset epoch, significant decoding was concentrated near the temporal diagonal, demonstrating that the resource-state representation evolved dynamically over time. By contrast, decoding generalized broadly across the wallet-update epoch, demonstrating a stable resource-state representation during this period. Within the offer-onset epoch, resource-state decoding was significantly stronger in ACC than in DLPFC ($p=\mbox{6.60e-34}$) or OFC ($p=\mbox{7.20e-34}$). \textbf{[TODO: specify the observation unit, pairing across regions, matrix entries included, test direction and correction for the two reported cross-region comparisons.] }Thus, resource-state representations were dynamic during the offer-onset epoch but stable during the wallet-update epoch, with ACC showing the strongest resource-state decoding during offer evaluation. Individual-animal analyses are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_cross_temporal}.

Trial averaging in the cross-temporal analysis could obscure the transition structure associated with each wallet update. We therefore projected population activity from $-100$ to $200$~ms relative to wallet update onto TDR-derived choice and resource-state axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}B). Earning drove activity toward the higher-resource-state direction in ACC and OFC, whereas spending drove it toward the lower-resource-state direction. To quantify these shifts, we regressed the resource-state-axis projection against time separately for earning and spending trials within each bootstrap iteration; the resulting temporal coefficient, $\beta_{\mathrm{update}}$, measured the direction and rate of movement along the resource-state axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}C). We defined choice-dependent resource-state updating as the joint presence of a significantly positive earning coefficient and a significantly negative spending coefficient. ACC and OFC satisfied this bidirectional criterion, whereas DLPFC did not in the pooled analysis. This pattern was broadly reproduced in individual animals, and the bidirectional effect was absent after shuffling either choice or resource-state labels (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_state_updating}). Thus, ACC and OFC not only represented current resource state but also updated that representation when the wallet changed, providing an updated state signal that could influence subsequent value coding.

\subsection{Resource state modulates prefrontal offer-value representations}
Having established that prefrontal populations represented and updated the current resource state, we next asked whether this state was incorporated into the neural processing of earning and spending values. The behavioral model predicted that diminishing marginal utility would cause earning values to decrease and converge as tokens accumulated, whereas spending values would increase while retaining the separation attributable to reward magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}A,B; see Methods). Representative OFC neurons exhibited response patterns consistent with these model predictions (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}A,B). We examined these predictions at the single-unit level by testing whether the signs of state-independent offer-value and resource-state coefficients were systematically associated. The predicted same-sign association for spending value was significant in all three regions, whereas the predicted opposite-sign association for earning value was not (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}C). The absence of an earning-value association may reflect the smaller number of neurons encoding earning value and the consequently limited sensitivity of this sign-based analysis.

To test for a state-dependent component beyond the independent effects of offers and holdings, we next used a nested-model comparison. Relative to a baseline model ($M_1$) containing state-independent offer values, categorical resource-state terms and choice, a second model ($M_2$) additionally included the resource-state-dependent earning value $V_E$ derived from the behavioral model (see Methods). Around wallet update, the increase in explained variance, $\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}$, was significantly greater than the value-label-shuffled control in ACC, DLPFC and OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}C). This increment supports a non-additive relationship between earning-offer information and resource state in single-unit activity around wallet update. An analogous nested-model test was not identifiable for $V_S$ because its state-dependent component was additive with resource state and was therefore absorbed by the resource-state terms already present in $M_1$ (see Methods).

We next used TDR to examine how this state dependence was expressed in a population value space defined by earning- and spending-value axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D). We quantified its geometry using the aspect ratio between the spending- and earning-axis ranges. Because the behavioral model predicted that earning values would converge while the separation among spending values would be preserved, this ratio was expected to increase with resource state. The aspect ratio increased monotonically in ACC and showed a weaker, non-monotonic tendency to be higher at high than at low resource states in DLPFC and OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}E). Resource-state-label shuffling attenuated this state-related structure, whereas value-label shuffling collapsed the underlying value-space geometry (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}), supporting the presence of a modest resource-state dependence in the population representation. Together, the single-unit analyses and population geometry indicate that resource state entered the representation of earning and spending opportunities around wallet update, with the sign associations, non-additive earning component and joint geometry capturing complementary aspects of this dependence.

\subsection{Decision-related value representations in prefrontal cortex}
The resource-state-dependent option values described above provide the inputs to a decision, raising the question of how they are transformed into a choice. A natural candidate for this transformation is the value difference, $\Delta V=V_E-V_S$, but the observed single-unit tuning did not support it as a dominant encoding property. Encoding $\Delta V$ would require opposite tuning directions for the two option values, predicting an enrichment of anti-diagonal combinations in their joint coefficient-sign matrices. Instead, the observed associations were diagonal, indicating same-sign rather than opposite-sign tuning (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}A).

An alternative is that value representations become linked to the selected option, as suggested by the chosen-value neurons described in OFC \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b}. Value tuning in our task was indeed choice dependent. In the representative neuron, firing increased with $V_E$ on both earning and spending trials, but with a steeper slope when earning was selected; $V_S$ tuning was positive when spending was selected but negative when earning was selected (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}A,B). At the population level, value encoding tended to be stronger when the encoded option was selected: $V_E$ encoding was enhanced on earning trials where this signal was evident, and $V_S$ encoding was enhanced on spending trials (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}C). These matched-condition advantages were smaller after choice-label shuffling (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_conditioned}).

To capture this relationship between value and the selected option, we defined a task-specific, bias-adjusted relative chosen-value measure, $V_{Chosen}=c(\Delta V-b)$, where $b$ is the fitted choice bias and $c$ denotes earning ($+1$) or spending ($-1$). This measure expresses the advantage of the selected option relative to the alternative and can be negative. $V_{Chosen}$ was encoded at both the single-unit (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}B) and population levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}D--F), with pooled population encoding strength following $V_S>V_{Chosen}>V_E$ in all three regions (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_area}). These signals also differed in their regional distribution: OFC represented all three value signals, whereas ACC showed little $V_E$ encoding during offer evaluation and DLPFC showed comparatively weak value encoding overall (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}D--F; Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_r2_by_variable}).

We next examined whether relative chosen-value activity developed between option-value and categorical choice signals. For signals with estimable latencies, half-peak and onset estimates were broadly consistent with the temporal progression $V_E/V_S\rightarrow V_{Chosen}\rightarrow\mathrm{Choice}$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}G--I and Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_value_latency} and \ref{tab:supp_value_onset}). The complete ordering was not equally resolved in every region, particularly given the limited offer-onset $V_E$ signal in ACC. These findings suggest a transformation from option-value representations toward choice, with $V_{Chosen}$ as a potential intermediate representation.

\subsection{Regional organization of good-based and action-based choice signals}
A choice in this task was expressed simultaneously in two coordinates: the identity of the selected option (earning or spending) and the spatial action used to report it (left or right). We therefore asked how the categorical choice signals identified above were organized across these good-based and action-based coordinates. The representative DLPFC unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}A primarily distinguished leftward from rightward responses, whereas the representative OFC unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}B primarily distinguished earning from spending choices.

We next used TDR to separate the two choice coordinates at the population level. Following offer onset, the four choice conditions separated most clearly along the earning/spending axis in OFC and along the left/right axis in DLPFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}C). The corresponding encoding trajectories confirmed distinct regional biases in choice format: DLPFC preferentially encoded the left/right action, OFC preferentially encoded the earning/spending identity of the selected option, and ACC showed a similar earning/spending bias but carried substantially weaker choice signals overall (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}D and Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_strength}). Thus, the same choice was represented in both coordinates across prefrontal cortex, but the relative prominence of these representations differed by region.

We then asked whether the coordinate encoded more strongly in each region also emerged earlier. No consistent temporal advantage was evident: the relative timing of earning/spending and left/right signals varied across regions and animals and differed between onset and half-peak estimates (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50}). Comparisons of each choice signal across regions likewise identified no brain area in which either signal consistently emerged first (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset_regions} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50_regions}). Regional preference in encoding strength was therefore not accompanied by a corresponding temporal priority, providing no consistent evidence for a fixed ordering of good-based and action-based choice signals across the three prefrontal regions.

Together, these results identify a regional organization of choice format, with earning/spending representations strongest in OFC, left/right representations strongest in DLPFC, and weaker choice signals in ACC with a relative earning/spending bias. This organization reflects differences in the strength with which the two choice coordinates were expressed, rather than a stable sequence in which the regionally preferred coordinate emerged first.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec3}

The value of an earning or spending opportunity depends on the resources already available. Our task made this dependence observable as monkeys accumulated tokens, spent them for water and retained unspent holdings across trials. Prefrontal activity represented current holdings, tracked their changes after outcomes and incorporated resource state into value-related responses. Relative chosen-value and categorical choice signals linked these representations to the selected option and its associated action. These findings extend the neural account of economic choice to decisions that continually change the resource state in which subsequent opportunities are evaluated.

Previous token studies established that accumulated holdings influence preferences, including choices involving token gains, losses and risk \citep{seo2009behavioral,yangPrimateAnteriorInsular2022c,ferroAccumulationVirtualTokens2025a}. Reward-saving tasks also demonstrated internally controlled decisions about when to consume accumulated rewards \citep{hernadi2015planning}. In those saving sequences, spending delivered the accumulated reward and ended the sequence. Here, spending could stop before the wallet was exhausted, allowing animals to return to earning at intermediate holdings. The absence of a fixed exchange threshold also reduced the coupling between holdings and proximity to an externally prescribed redemption goal. Crossing earning rates with spending reward magnitudes then exposed the joint representation of two distinct opportunities across the resource states generated by the animal's own choices. The contribution is therefore not simply that holdings affect preferences, but that resource acquisition and partial consumption can be examined within a shared, continuously changing decision context.

Classic OFC recordings identified subjective offer-value representations \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b}; our results show how such representations relate to currently available resources. Single-unit sign associations, the incremental earning-value component and population geometry provided complementary evidence for this dependence around wallet update. The incremental component was less evident at offer onset, suggesting that state-dependent value processing may vary across task stages. % TODO: cite the restored two-stage supplementary comparison after the artwork is updated.
 The measurements do not by themselves establish where or how resource and offer information are combined. In particular, the behavioral model supplies a computational account and predictions for neural analysis rather than a uniquely established valuation mechanism. The precise functional form through which resource state affects value remains a question for future systematic investigation.

At the single-unit level, opposite tuning directions for earning and spending values were not enriched as expected for a dominant value-difference representation. Instead, value tuning depended on the selected option, motivating the relative chosen-value measure $V_{Chosen}$. Classic chosen-value studies defined the value of the selected reward on a common scale and evaluated it against alternative candidate variables \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b}. Our measure differs: it is the bias-adjusted advantage of the selected option over its alternative. This definition retains the relative information relevant to the earning--spending decision while making its dependence on choice explicit. Its temporal development between detectable option-value and categorical choice signals suggests a potential intermediate representation, without establishing an independent computational stage.

Such a representation could participate in the evolving competition between options rather than require a separate operation after a choice has already been made. Circuit accounts of OFC similarly allow choice-related activity to develop during competition \citep{ballesta2019inhibition}. Selective attention offers another possible contribution, because OFC value responses vary with fixation location \citep{mcginty2016fixation}. These interpretations distinguish the observed temporal ordering from a claim that each signal marks a discrete processing stage. The present data establish the single-unit encoding patterns and their population timing; they leave open how the relative chosen-value signal contributes to resolving the decision.

The regional distribution further separates resource context from the representation of particular options. ACC strongly represented current holdings and their updates, but showed little earning-value encoding during offer evaluation and comparatively weak choice signals. Its state-dependent value-space geometry was measured around wallet update, so it concerns a different task stage from the limited offer-onset earning signal. OFC combined resource representation and updating with both option values, relative chosen value and good-based choice. This combination connects valuation to the broader proposal that OFC represents task state \citep{schuck2016state}. Comparisons with resource signals in instructed token tasks also depend on the population measure: Nguyen and colleagues distinguished distributed single-neuron reference signals from homogeneous population coding in ventral-bank ACC \citep{nguyenNeuralRepresentationDecisional2025a}. Decoding resource state from heterogeneous activity addresses a different aspect of its representation. Our results suggest regional differences in emphasis rather than an exclusive assignment of resource processing to one area.

DLPFC emphasized action-based choice, whereas OFC emphasized good-based choice, but neither preference established a consistent temporal priority. Earlier work separating offer presentation from the revelation of response targets demonstrated a good-to-action transformation in lateral prefrontal cortex \citep{cai2014contributions}. When option identity and location are available together, however, action-based value processing can participate in decision formation \citep{lin2021evidence}. Our findings support coexistence of both choice formats without resolving a fixed serial order across regions. Within-trial switching poses a related limitation: switching options requires a saccade, whereas staying requires continued fixation. These events therefore cannot isolate switching-related activity from movement-related activity. A task requiring a renewed fixation and an explicit response on every decision would help separate these contributions.

Several model and measurement boundaries guide further work. The behavioral model assumes constant utility per unit of water and does not explicitly include fixation or effort costs. Incorporating intake-dependent water utility and duration-dependent fixation costs could extend the account. Displayed token count also covaries with resource quantity, so varying token denominations or requiring holdings to be inferred would distinguish visual numerosity from an abstract resource representation. Although eliminating a fixed redemption threshold removes one external reference point, it does not determine whether animals use an internal reference. These questions concern the interpretation and generalization of resource-dependent valuation beyond the current task.

Finally, pseudo-population analyses reveal representational structure but do not resolve trial-by-trial interactions between regions. Simultaneous recordings and temporally targeted perturbations could test whether changing a resource representation alters subsequent valuation and earning--spending choice. OFC stimulation studies demonstrate that perturbing value processing can change economic decisions \citep{ballestaValuesEncodedOrbitofrontal2020a,ballestaOrbitofrontalCortexContributes2022}; extending this approach to resource-dependent choice would help distinguish state estimation from valuation and action selection. The present task provides a basis for these questions by linking the resources an animal retains to the neural representation of its next opportunities.

\section{Methods}\label{sec4}

\subsection{Subjects}
Three male rhesus monkeys (\textit{Macaca mulatta}; monkeys W, U and T) were used. Their body weights during the experiments were 6.3--8.0, 3.5--4.6 and 5.8--6.7~kg, respectively. All procedures were approved by the relevant institutional animal-care committee. \textbf{[TODO: provide the committee's current official English name and protocol number.]}

\subsection{Experimental apparatus}
Experiments were conducted in a sound-attenuated, dimly lit room maintained at 20--25$^\circ$C. Monkeys sat in a custom primate chair with the head fixed and viewed a 23.6-inch monitor at a distance of approximately 60~cm. Visual stimulus presentation and reward delivery were controlled using MonkeyLogic~2 running in MATLAB. Eye position was sampled at 1,000~Hz using an EyeLink~1000 infrared eye tracker. Water was delivered through a computer-controlled solenoid valve calibrated at approximately 0.2~$\mu$l~ms$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Behavioral task}
Monkeys performed an earning--spending task (Figs.~\ref{fig:behavior}A,~B) in which tokens accumulated across trials and could subsequently be spent for water. Current holdings were displayed by a central ``wallet'', a $5\times5$ circle array approximately $4.3^\circ$ wide. Filled circles indicated the number of tokens currently held. The wallet remained visible during the inter-trial interval (ITI), when the option stimuli were absent, and updated immediately after each successful earning or spending event. Holdings were bounded between 0 and 25 tokens. Earning at 25 tokens did not add another token, and spending at 0 tokens did not remove one, although the attempted choice was recorded. Each session began at 0 tokens.

Each trial presented one earning stimulus and one spending stimulus. The two stimuli appeared at $6^\circ$ eccentricity, at locations rotated $90^\circ$ clockwise and counter-clockwise from the monkey's gaze direction at trial onset. Assignment of earning and spending stimuli to the two locations was randomized across trials. The earning rate and spending reward were sampled independently with equal probability, yielding 12 offer conditions. The three earning rates were one token per 250, 200 or 100~ms of continuous fixation (4, 5 or 10 tokens~s$^{-1}$). Spending always removed one token per 200~ms (5 tokens~s$^{-1}$) and delivered the reward associated with the spending stimulus at each removal. For monkeys W and U, solenoid-open durations were 0, 20, 35 and 55~ms per token; for monkey T, they were 15, 25, 35 and 70~ms per token.

Monkeys selected an option by directing gaze to it and maintaining fixation. Continued fixation could generate multiple token updates, and monkeys could switch between the two options before trial end. Trial duration was drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean of 3,000~ms and truncated to 1,000--5,000~ms; the ITI was approximately 1~s. We define \emph{offer onset} as the simultaneous appearance of the two option stimuli, an \emph{initial choice} as the first option fixation that successfully produced an earning or spending event, a \emph{wallet update} as an individual token addition or deduction, and a \emph{choice switch} as a transition to the other option in which both the preceding and subsequent fixations produced a token event and the inter-fixation interval did not exceed 150~ms.

\subsection{Training}
Following acquisition of a standard delayed saccade task, monkeys were progressively trained on the earning--spending task. Reward magnitude ($V_\text{Spend}$) was initially set high to ensure adequate daily water intake, then gradually reduced as each monkey's choice behavior converged to a stable pattern. Full training was completed in approximately 6 months for all subjects.

\subsection{Surgery and recording-site localization}
Before behavioral training, each monkey underwent surgery to implant a headpost for head fixation. After behavior stabilized, an acrylic recording chamber was implanted over prefrontal cortex. The chamber's inner dimensions were 25~mm along the anterior--posterior axis, 20~mm along the medial--lateral axis and 10~mm along the superior--inferior axis. Chambers were placed over the left hemisphere in monkeys W and T and the right hemisphere in monkey U. Bone within the chamber was removed while leaving the dura intact. All surgeries were conducted under aseptic conditions. Anesthesia was induced with ketamine hydrochloride (5--15~mg~kg$^{-1}$, i.m.) and maintained with 1.5--2\% isoflurane; heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature and end-tidal CO$_2$ were monitored continuously.

After chamber implantation, structural MRI was acquired using a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3-T scanner. A 1~mol~l$^{-1}$ manganese chloride tetrahydrate stock solution was diluted 66-fold and placed in blind holes of the recording grid as a contrast marker. Grid holes were 1~mm in diameter, spaced 2~mm apart and aligned with the electrode-penetration axis. Recording locations were assigned using the spatial correspondence between the contrast-filled grid and brain anatomy together with physiological landmarks encountered during penetrations, including changes in spiking density and impedance. ACC recordings corresponded approximately to BA~24, OFC to BA~11 and DLPFC to BA~46; their locations and anterior--posterior extents are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_recording_sites}.

\subsection{Neurophysiology}
Neuronal activity was recorded using 16-channel Plexon S-Probes (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, USA). Adjacent recording contacts were separated by 100~$\mu$m, such that the 16 contacts spanned 1.5~mm along the probe shaft. Probes were advanced to the target depth using an EPS multichannel microdrive (Alpha Omega Engineering, Nof HaGalil, Israel). Signals were acquired using an AlphaLab SnR system (Alpha Omega Engineering) at 44~kHz and band-pass filtered online between 300 and 6,000~Hz. Electrophysiological data were collected over 52 recording sessions in monkey W, 63 in monkey U and 64 in monkey T (179 sessions in total). In total, we recorded 679 units from ACC (247 from monkey W and 432 from T; no ACC recordings were obtained from U), 1,056 from DLPFC (127, 491 and 438 from W, U and T) and 903 from OFC (111, 446 and 346 from W, U and T; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_recording_sites}).

\subsection{Behavioral analysis and computational modeling}

All behavioral sessions in which electrophysiological data were recorded were included. Throughout the Methods, \emph{token holdings} denotes the number of tokens displayed in the wallet at the task event specified for each analysis and is represented by $W$ in equations. Token holdings operationalized the animal's current resource state; the term \emph{wealth-like} is reserved for the broader interpretation of this task variable. Behavioral analyses used only the initial successful choice on each trial, thereby avoiding dependence introduced by repeated within-trial selections. Trials were excluded if the initial fixation did not produce an earning or spending event. Observations at the wallet boundaries (0 or 25 tokens) were also excluded because one option could not change the resource state. Consequently, all behavioral value models were evaluated over holdings of 1--24 tokens.

\subsubsection{Non-parametric weight estimation}
To quantify the modulation of option value by token holdings without assuming a specific functional form, we estimated subjective weights for the seven shapes (three earning, four spending) at each of the 24 token-holding levels. Earning probability was modeled by a sigmoid function:
\begin{equation}
P_\text{earn} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\bigl(-(V_{e,w} - V_{s,w})\bigr)},
\end{equation}
where $V_{e,w}$ and $V_{s,w}$ are the estimated weights of the earning and spending options at token-holding level $w$. To regularize the solution and encourage gradual variation across adjacent levels, we augmented the cross-entropy loss with an $L_2$ smoothing penalty:
\begin{align}
L = & -\sum_{i} \bigl[c_i \ln \hat{p}_i + (1 - c_i) \ln(1 - \hat{p}_i)\bigr] \\
& + \lambda \sum_{e,s,w} \bigl(V_{e/s,w} - V_{e/s,w+1}\bigr)^2,
\end{align}
where $c_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary choice (1 = earn, 0 = spend), $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted earning probability, and $\lambda = 10$ controls the smoothing strength. Parameters were estimated by unconstrained optimization (\texttt{fminunc}, quasi-Newton algorithm, MATLAB).

Numerical optimization settings are given in Supplementary Methods.

The model contained no separate bias term or fixed reference option. Choice probabilities depend on differences between option weights; a common constant offset leaves both the likelihood and the smoothing penalty unchanged. Interpretation therefore concerns relative weights and their state dependence rather than an absolute value baseline.

\subsubsection{Parametric utility model}
We developed a parametric model based on marginal utility theory. We assumed a power-law utility function $U(w) = w^\alpha$, where $w$ denotes current holdings and $\alpha$ is the curvature exponent. Reward magnitude was expressed in dimensionless units by assigning the second-smallest magnitude a value of 1 and scaling the remaining magnitudes proportionally. Overall reward scaling was absorbed by the fitted token--reward exchange-rate parameter $ER$. The subjective values of earning and spending were defined as:
\begin{align}
V_E &= \frac{ES}{ER} \cdot \alpha w^{\alpha-1}, \\
V_S &= R \cdot SS - \frac{SS}{ER} \cdot \alpha w^{\alpha-1},
\end{align}
where $ES$ is the earning speed (tokens/s), $R$ is the normalized reward magnitude (in arbitrary units), $ER$ is the exchange rate (tokens per unit reward), and $SS = 5$ tokens/s is the spend speed. The first term in $V_S$ represents the immediate reward benefit; the second captures the opportunity cost of spending a token scaled by its marginal utility. Throughout, $\Delta V=V_E-V_S$ denotes the unnormalized value difference. Choice probability was given by:
\begin{equation}
P_\text{earn} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\left[-(\Delta V-b)/\gamma\right]},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma > 0$ is a choice-noise scale (smaller values imply greater sensitivity) and $b$ is a decision bias. Neural analyses of value-difference encoding used the equivalent unnormalized difference $V_E-V_S$; subtracting a constant bias and applying a positive scale factor do not alter the corresponding regression inference because these transformations are absorbed by the intercept and coefficient magnitude, respectively. Free parameters $(\alpha,\, ER,\, b,\, \gamma)$ were estimated by minimizing cross-entropy loss using \texttt{fmincon} (SQP algorithm, MATLAB) with MultiStart (10 random initializations). Initial exploratory fits allowed $\alpha\geq0$; because all estimates were below 1, subsequent fits constrained $\alpha$ to $[0,1]$ for numerical stability. The marginal-utility expression was evaluated only for $w=1,\ldots,24$; observations at $w=0$ were excluded as described above.

The \textit{fixed-$\alpha$} model served as a nested baseline in which $\alpha$ was constrained to 1, corresponding to linear utility and constant marginal utility. All remaining parameters were estimated freely, testing whether state dependence improved prediction within this model family. Cross-validation, session-wise parameter estimation, model-prediction visualization and reaction-time analyses are described in Supplementary Methods.

\subsection{Neural data preprocessing}

\subsubsection{Spike sorting and active-epoch selection}
Spike sorting was performed automatically using Kilosort4. A subset of automatically sorted units was compared with online sorting and showed concordant active periods, waveforms and firing rates; no additional manual curation was applied. To exclude silent periods caused by electrode drift, loss of isolation or cell injury, firing rate was evaluated in non-overlapping 60-s windows. For each unit, only the longest continuous sequence of windows with a mean firing rate above 1~Hz was retained. The resulting active-epoch boundaries were rounded inward to trial boundaries, such that only complete trials lying within the active epoch were included; no temporal padding was applied.

\subsubsection{Firing-rate estimation and representative-neuron PSTHs}
Spike trains were initially converted to firing rates in 10-ms bins. Analyses based on a single mean firing rate within a predefined window used unsmoothed rates. PSTHs and other time-resolved trajectories were smoothed with a causal Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 100$~ms; half-window = 300~ms). The kernel spanned only $[-300,0]$~ms relative to each time point, so the smoothed rate depended exclusively on preceding spikes.

\subsection{Targeted dimensionality reduction}
We employed TDR to project population firing patterns into a low-dimensional subspace aligned to task-relevant variables.

\subsubsection{Unit filtering}
Neurons were filtered according to each analysis. For the primary TDR analyses, a unit was retained if it contributed at least 50 valid trials in total and at least 10 valid trials to every required condition group.

\subsubsection{Regression models}
Firing rates at each time bin were z-scored independently: the mean and standard deviation across training trials were computed at each time bin and applied to normalize both training and test responses, preventing test-set information from entering the fitted model. Task variables were likewise z-scored within the training partition. The regression target for each neuron was the z-scored firing rate averaged over the analysis-specific regression window, yielding one scalar observation per trial. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was then applied to estimate each neuron's coefficient vector $\boldsymbol{\beta}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{V}$; these were stacked row-wise to form the population coefficient matrix $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times V}$.

For the results in Figures~\ref{fig:choice_representation}, we used the following model (regression window: 0--500~ms aligned to offer onset; projection window: -200--500~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \sum_v \beta_v V_v + \beta_{W}\,W + \sum_c \beta_c Choice_c + \beta_0
\end{equation}
where $V_v \in \{V_L, V_R, V_E, V_S\}$ denote the values of the spatially-mapped and task-defined options, $W$ is the token count at offer onset, and $Choice_c \in \{Choice_{LR}, Choice_{ES}\}$ represents the monkey's choices in the action and good-based domains. Because $V_L$/$V_R$ and $V_E$/$V_S$ are collinear by construction, ridge regression ($\lambda = 5$) replaced OLS for this model to stabilize coefficient estimation; all other models used standard OLS.

For the results in Figure~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics} (regression window: $-100$--200~ms relative to token update; projection window: $-100$--200~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1\,V_E + \beta_2\,V_S + \beta_3\,Choice_{ES} + \beta_4\,W
\end{equation}

For the population value-space analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D,E, firing rates were averaged from $-200$ to $300$~ms relative to resource-state update and regressed using the same window:
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_C\,Choice + \beta_W\,W + \beta_E\,V_E + \beta_S\,V_S.
\end{equation}
Here $Choice$ denotes earning versus spending, $W$ is the continuous token count at resource-state update, and $V_E$ and $V_S$ are the resource-state-dependent earning and spending values derived from the free-$\alpha$ behavioral model. The regression coefficient matrix and subsequent QR decomposition used the common column order $[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{Choice},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{W},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_E},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_S}]$. The third and fourth columns of the resulting orthonormal basis defined the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D.

To examine value encoding conditional on choice (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}C), trials were partitioned by the monkey's behavioral output (earning vs.\ spending) and independent TDR models were fit within each condition (regression window: 0--500~ms post-offer; projection window: $-200$--500~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1\,V_E + \beta_2\,V_S + \beta_3\,W
\end{equation}
The coefficient matrix was orthogonalized via QR decomposition with column ordering $[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{W},\, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_E},\, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_S}]$, yielding encoding axes for $V_E$ and $V_S$ residualized against token-holding effects. Test trials were stratified into tertiles of the corresponding value variable (100 trials per tertile). The analysis was repeated over 100 bootstrap iterations, each sampling units (100 per individual subject; 300 for the pooled analysis; replacement procedure to be verified below).

To compare value and choice encoding and their temporal emergence (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}D--I), a joint model was fit over the offer-onset epoch (regression window: 0--500~ms; projection window: $-200$--500~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1\,W + \beta_2\,Choice + \beta_3\,V_E + \beta_4\,V_S + \beta_5\,V_{Chosen}
\end{equation}
Four separate encoding axes were extracted by repeating QR decomposition with the target variable placed last in the column ordering, ensuring each axis is orthogonal to all others. Test trials were stratified into two groups for the choice axis (150 trials per group) or three tertile groups for value axes (100 trials per group).

\subsubsection{Ordered QR orthonormalization and projection}
The coefficient matrix $\mathbf{B}$ was orthonormalized via QR decomposition:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}
\end{equation}
where the columns of $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times V}$ form an orthonormal basis of task-related population axes. QR decomposition was treated as an ordered variance partition: earlier variables occupied shared coefficient space first, and each later axis represented the component remaining after the preceding variables. For target-specific encoding analyses, QR decomposition was repeated with the target variable placed last, so that the target axis was residualized against all other regressors. For the joint value-space analysis, a single ordered basis $[Choice,W,V_E,V_S]$ was retained. Thus, the $V_E$ axis was residualized against choice and token holdings, whereas the $V_S$ axis was additionally residualized against $V_E$. These two axes define a common ordered value space and were not treated as exchange-symmetric estimates for an unconditional comparison of the absolute magnitudes of $V_E$ and $V_S$ encoding. Time-resolved population signals were obtained by projecting the z-scored test-set firing-rate matrix $\mathbf{X}(t)$ onto the basis:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}(t) = \mathbf{Q}^T \mathbf{X}(t)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Cross-validation and resampling}
To obtain stable estimates of encoding strength and characterize uncertainty, the regression--projection pipeline was repeated over $N_{CV}$ iterations (10 for cross-temporal decoding and 100 for other TDR analyses). In each iteration, units were sampled (100 per individual monkey; 300 for pooled pseudo-populations), and a fresh 50/50 trial partition was drawn independently for each unit. The training set was used to fit $\mathbf{B}_p$ and derive $\mathbf{Q}_p$; test-set firing rates were then projected onto $\mathbf{Q}_p$. Test trials were assigned to condition groups and resampled. A fixed total of 300 trials was drawn, distributed evenly across groups, and pooled with the corresponding labels. Encoding strength at each time point was the squared Pearson correlation between projection and group label:
\begin{equation}
R^2(t) = \left[\mathrm{corr}\!\left(\mathbf{p}(t),\, \mathbf{l}\right)\right]^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}(t)$ is the vector of pooled projections at time $t$ and $\mathbf{l}$ the corresponding group label vector. The mean and standard deviation of $R^2(t)$ across $N_{CV}$ iterations are reported throughout.

Resampling iterations reuse the recorded data and are not statistically independent observations or biological replicates. Their distributions summarize the stability of pseudo-population estimates under repeated sampling and trial partitioning. Tests across iterations describe differences between these resampling distributions, including comparisons with matched baseline or shuffled estimates; they should not be interpreted as inference from 100 independent animals or sessions. Individual-animal results are reported separately. \textbf{[TODO: verify unit sampling with or without replacement, test-trial sampling with or without replacement, and analysis-specific exceptions against the code; apply the verified description consistently to all TDR analyses.]}

The value-space analysis used the same training/test separation and repeated sampling. Geometry summaries used all 100 iterations; selection of the illustrative projection and its separation from quantification are described in Supplementary Methods.

\subsubsection{Cross-temporal decoding framework}
To assess the temporal stability of encoded variables across task epochs, we performed cross-temporal decoding for both token holdings and the value variables ($V_E$, $V_S$, $V_{Chosen}$). Time bins were defined at 50~ms resolution. For token holdings, 14 bins spanned the offer-onset epoch ($-200$ to $500$~ms) and 10 bins spanned the wallet-update epoch ($-200$ to $300$~ms), yielding a $24 \times 24$ decoding matrix; for the value variables, 10 bins spanned the offer-onset epoch ($0$ to $500$~ms) and 10 bins the peri-choice epoch ($-200$ to $300$~ms), yielding a $20 \times 20$ matrix. At each training bin, a TDR axis was fitted following the regression and QR orthogonalization procedures described above and used to decode the target variable at every other bin, with $R^2$ computed from tertile-stratified test trials. The procedure was repeated for $N_{CV} = 10$ bootstrap iterations; the reduced number of iterations reflects the computational cost of the full cross-temporal design. In each iteration, units were sampled (100 per individual subject; 300 for the pooled analysis; replacement procedure to be verified above) and a fresh trial partition was drawn independently per unit. The mean $R^2$ across iterations is reported.

Each matrix entry was tested against a shuffle control in which variable labels were permuted across trials within each unit. Statistical significance was assessed using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test comparing real and shuffle $R^2$ values across bootstrap iterations. Entries not significantly exceeding the shuffle distribution are displayed in black.

\subsubsection{Latency analysis}
Two latency metrics were derived from each $R^2(t)$ trajectory. The \emph{onset latency}, reported in Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_value_onset}, was defined as the onset of the first sustained cluster exceeding the 95th percentile of the pre-offer baseline distribution ($[-200,0)$~ms). The \emph{half-peak time} ($t_{50}$), reported in Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}G--I, was the first time in $[0,T_{\mathrm{peak}}]$ at which $R^2(t)$ reached the midpoint between the baseline median and response peak. \subsection{Resource-state representation and updating}
\subsubsection{Single-unit resource-state selectivity and tuning}
To quantify token-holding selectivity, we regressed the mean firing rate in a window of $-300$ to $300$~ms relative to offer onset against task variables:
\begin{equation}
    FR \sim \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 Choice_{ES} + \beta_4 W + \beta_0
    \label{eq:cat_wealth}
\end{equation}
where $W$ denotes the token count at offer onset, treated as a categorical variable with five levels ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15], [16--24]). A logarithmic binning scheme was used to define these levels, yielding more comparable trial counts across categories and accounting for the natural distribution of token-holding states encountered during the task. Neurons were classified as token-holding selective if the $F$-test for the $W$ term reached significance ($\alpha = 0.05$). The preferred token-holding level was defined as the level yielding the smallest $p$-value across pairwise two-sample $t$-tests, each comparing the mean firing rate of one token-holding group against all remaining groups combined. The direction of tuning was determined by the sign of $\bar{r}_{pref} - \bar{r}_{rest}$. For population averaging, the tuning curves of negatively tuned neurons ($\bar{r}_{pref} - \bar{r}_{rest} < 0$) were inverted prior to normalization. Min-max normalization was then applied:
\begin{equation}
    TC_{norm} = \frac{TC - \min(TC)}{\max(TC) - \min(TC)}
\end{equation}
Normalized curves were averaged across neurons sharing the same preferred token-holding level to yield group-level population tuning curves. To characterize the shape of the re-centered population tuning curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}I--K), the normalized firing rates at relative positions $\pm1$ and $\pm2$ from each neuron's preferred token-holding level were extracted and compared using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Under the null hypothesis that the tuning curves reflect random noise, firing rates at all non-peak positions would be indistinguishable from one another; a significant difference between positions $\pm1$ and $\pm2$ therefore provides evidence for a genuine, graded decay away from the response peak. This interpretation is corroborated by a shuffle control (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}), in which token-holding group labels were randomly permuted across trials prior to all analyses, confirming that the observed gradient structure cannot be attributed to chance.

Shuffle and individual-animal procedures are detailed in Supplementary Methods.

\subsubsection{Cross-temporal resource-state decoding}
The cross-temporal procedure described above was applied to resource state across 14 offer-aligned 50-ms bins ($-200$ to 500~ms) and 10 wallet-update-aligned 50-ms bins ($-200$ to 300~ms), producing a $24\times24$ matrix. Resource state was divided into five groups ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15] and [16--24]); 60 test trials were resampled per group. Each matrix entry was compared with a label-shuffled control using a paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over matched resampling iterations. The complete analysis was repeated separately for each monkey. \textbf{[TODO: specify the observation unit, pairing, included matrix entries, test direction and correction used for the reported cross-region comparisons within the offer-onset quadrant.]}

\subsubsection{Choice-dependent resource-state updating}
For wallet-update trajectories, mean firing rate from $-100$ to 200~ms relative to each update was regressed on $V_E$, $V_S$, earning/spending choice and resource state. The QR order was chosen so that the plotted choice and resource-state axes were residualized against the remaining regressors. \textbf{[TODO: provide the exact QR column order and confirm whether resource state in this regression denotes the pre-update or post-update token count.]}
Test-set population activity was projected from $-100$ to 200~ms onto the resulting axes. Within each resampling iteration, projection onto the resource-state axis was regressed against time separately for earning and spending updates; the temporal coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{update}}$ was positive for movement toward higher represented wealth and negative for movement toward lower represented wealth. Earning coefficients were tested against zero with a right-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test and spending coefficients with a left-tailed test. Choice-dependent updating required both tests to be significant. \subsection{Resource-state modulation of offer-value representations}

\subsubsection{Time-resolved nested-model comparison}
Spike trains were smoothed with the causal Gaussian kernel described above and averaged in non-overlapping 100-ms windows spanning $-200$ to 800~ms relative to offer onset and $-500$ to 500~ms relative to wallet update. The update-aligned analysis was performed both with all valid update events and after randomly subsampling, within unit, to match the number of valid offer-onset trials. Repeated updates within a trial could contribute overlapping event-aligned windows; this analysis quantified activity around updates rather than isolated responses to non-overlapping events.

Two nested linear models were fitted in each window. The baseline model ($M_1$) incorporated resource-state-independent offer values, choice direction and 23 wealth-level indicators (wealth 1 as the reference):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3\,\mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k\,\mathbf{1}[W = k].
\end{equation}
where $V_i^{\mathrm{obj}}$ denotes state-independent offer predictors from the fixed-$\alpha$ model, which are affine functions of objective earning rate and spending reward magnitude. They do not contain token-holding modulation. The token-holding-modulated model ($M_2$) extended $M_1$ with the free-$\alpha$ earning value as an additional regressor:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{FR} \sim & \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3\,\mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k\,\mathbf{1}[W = k] \\
& + \beta_4\,V_{E,w}.
\end{align}
An analogous increment could not be estimated for resource-dependent spending value. With a fixed spending rate, $V_S$ is the sum of a state-independent reward term and a token-cost term that depends only on $W$; these components were already represented by the spending-value and resource-state regressors in $M_1$, making an additional $V_S$ regressor exactly collinear with the baseline model.
Model performance was quantified by adjusted $R^2$ to penalize the additional parameter in $M_2$. The modulation index $\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}(M_2)-R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}(M_1)$ was computed for every unit and time window. The null analysis permuted the state-dependent $V_E$ labels across trials and refitted both models. Observed and shuffled per-unit increments were compared using a paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. \textbf{[TODO: specify the precise within-unit strata used when permuting $V_E$, the number of permutations and correction across time windows.]}

\subsubsection{Resource-state-dependent geometry of offer-value representations}
We used the $V_E$ and $V_S$ TDR axes defined above to visualize the joint population representation of earning and spending offers around resource-state update. The task contained 12 offer conditions formed by crossing three earning rates with four spending reward magnitudes. The zero-reward spending option was rarely chosen and therefore provided too few update-aligned observations for stable condition averages. We excluded this condition from the geometry analysis, leaving the $3\times3$ subset formed by the three earning rates and three non-zero spending rewards. Resource state was divided into low (1 token), middle (2--5 tokens) and high (6--24 tokens) groups. Within each brain area and resource-state group, test-set activity was averaged over $-200$ to $300$~ms relative to resource-state update and then averaged separately for each of the nine offer conditions, yielding nine condition-averaged population states in the $V_E$--$V_S$ space. Lines connecting neighboring conditions in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D were included only to display the adjacency structure of the $3\times3$ offer matrix and were not used in any calculation.

The aspect ratio was the trimmed spending-axis range divided by the trimmed earning-axis range, calculated separately for each resource-state group and iteration. Exact range definitions, visualization selection and label-shuffle procedures are given in Supplementary Methods. The complete analysis was repeated for every animal with recordings in each region.

\subsubsection{Joint-encoding analysis}
To characterize the co-representation of offer value and token holdings, the following regression was fitted separately to each unit's mean unsmoothed firing rate during offer onset (0--500~ms) and wallet update ($-300$ to 300~ms):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3\,\mathrm{Choice} + \beta_4\,W,
\end{equation}
where $W$ was entered as a continuous linear predictor and objective (fixed-$\alpha$) values were used to ensure that its coefficient captured token-holding-related firing variance orthogonal to value encoding. A unit was classified as positively or negatively selective for a given variable if the corresponding regression coefficient was significant ($p < 0.05$); otherwise it was excluded from the contingency for that variable pair. Only units simultaneously selective for both value and token holdings were entered into the $2\times2$ contingency table. For earning value, a left-tailed Fisher's exact test was applied, based on the a priori directional prediction that diminishing marginal utility produces an anti-diagonal pattern (VE$+$/Token$-$). For spending value, a right-tailed Fisher's exact test was used, reflecting the complementary prediction that higher token holdings increase subjective spending value, producing a diagonal pattern (VS$+$/Token$+$).

\subsection{Decision-related value and choice representations}
\subsubsection{Single-unit good-based and action-based choice selectivity}
For each neuron, firing rates were averaged over the offer period (0--500~ms post-onset) to yield a single response scalar per trial. These mean rates were regressed against the monkey's binary choice in each domain:
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot Choice_{LR} + \beta_2 \cdot Choice_{ES}.
\end{equation}
Coefficient significance classified units as selective in the action and/or good-based domains. Relative encoding was quantified from $|\beta_{ES}|$ and $|\beta_{LR}|$. For each unit, $d=(|\beta_{ES}|-|\beta_{LR}|)/\sqrt{2}$ measured displacement along the anti-diagonal of the coefficient plane. A two-sided binomial test on the sign of $d$ tested whether an area contained more units biased toward one reference frame than expected under a 0.5 null proportion. Spearman rank correlation quantified the association between $|\beta_{ES}|$ and $|\beta_{LR}|$ across units.

\subsubsection{Joint encoding of $V_E$ and $V_S$}
To test whether single units preferentially encoded the value difference $\Delta V = V_E - V_S$, we fit an OLS regression model to mean firing rates in the offer-onset epoch ($[0, 500]$~ms) and the token-update epoch ($[0, 150]$~ms):
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 \, \mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k \, \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{token} = k],
\end{equation*}
where token holdings were included as a 23-level dummy-coded categorical variable (1 token as reference). A unit was classified as $V_E$-positive ($V_{E,\mathrm{pos}}$) if $\beta_1 > 0$ and $p(\beta_1) < 0.05$, as $V_E$-negative ($V_{E,\mathrm{neg}}$) if $\beta_1 < 0$ and $p(\beta_1) < 0.05$, and non-selective otherwise; the analogous classification was applied to $\beta_2$ for $V_S$. Among units significant for both $V_E$ and $V_S$, we constructed a $2 \times 2$ contingency table (rows: $V_E$ sign; columns: $V_S$ sign) and applied a two-sided Fisher's exact test to assess sign association. We inspected its direction to determine whether the anti-diagonal cells ($V_{E,\mathrm{pos}} \times V_{S,\mathrm{neg}}$ and $V_{E,\mathrm{neg}} \times V_{S,\mathrm{pos}}$) were over-represented, as would be expected if $\Delta V$ were the dominant encoded quantity.

\subsubsection{Relative chosen-value selectivity}
The behavioral model includes a bias parameter $b$ representing the animal's indifference point between earning and spending. This parameter shifts the choice rule on $\Delta V$. We defined the task-specific, bias-adjusted relative chosen-value measure $V_{Chosen}$ as
\begin{equation*}
    V_{Chosen} = c(\Delta V-b),
\end{equation*}
where $c=+1$ for earning choices and $c=-1$ for spending choices. This variable is the signed value advantage of the chosen option over the unchosen option relative to the fitted indifference point; it is not the absolute subjective value of the selected offer. The regression model was
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i + \beta_4 \, \mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k \, \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{token} = k],
\end{equation*}
where $V_i\in\{V_E,V_S,V_{Chosen}\}$, fit to mean unsmoothed firing rates in the offer-onset epoch (0--500~ms). Token holdings were represented by 23 indicators for holdings 2--24, with 1 token as the reference. Units with $p(\beta_{V_{Chosen}})<0.05$ were classified as $V_{Chosen}$-selective. The empirical false-positive proportion was estimated from 10 repetitions in which trial labels were randomly permuted before refitting the complete model. \textbf{[TODO: specify which labels were permuted jointly and whether permutation was restricted within unit or other trial strata.]}

\subsubsection{Choice-conditioned value encoding}
To test whether the represented value depended on the selected option, trials were divided into earning and spending choices and independent TDR models were fitted within each pool:
\begin{equation}
FR\sim\beta_0+\beta_E V_E+\beta_S V_S+\beta_W W.
\end{equation}
The regression window was 0--500~ms after offer onset and the projection window was $-200$ to 500~ms. QR ordering was $[W,V_E,V_S]$. Test trials were divided into tertiles of the value being decoded, with 100 trials resampled per tertile. Choice dependence was summarized by
\begin{equation}
D=[R^2(V_E\mid Earn)-R^2(V_S\mid Earn)]-[R^2(V_E\mid Spend)-R^2(V_S\mid Spend)].
\end{equation}
Within each of 100 iterations, $R^2(t)$ was averaged over the predefined 0--500-ms post-offer window. We calculated $\Delta R^2_{V_E}=R^2(V_E\mid Earn)-R^2(V_E\mid Spend)$ and $\Delta R^2_{V_S}=R^2(V_S\mid Spend)-R^2(V_S\mid Earn)$ and tested each against zero using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test in the predefined positive direction.
The complete analysis was repeated for individual monkeys and after choice-label shuffling (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_choice_conditioned}).

\subsubsection{Temporal ordering of option value, relative chosen value and choice}
Temporal encoding of $V_E$, $V_S$, $V_{Chosen}$ and categorical earning/spending choice was estimated using the joint regression described in the TDR section, repeating QR with each target placed last. Choice trials were divided into two groups (150 resampled trials per group), whereas continuous value variables were divided into tertiles (100 per group). Onset latency and $t_{50}$ were computed and compared as specified above. Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_value_latency} and \ref{tab:supp_value_onset} report half-peak and onset latencies, respectively, and the fraction of resampling iterations yielding valid estimates.

\subsubsection{Good-based and action-based choice formats}
Single-unit good-based and action-based selectivity was quantified as described above. At the population level, TDR used the joint model containing spatially mapped values $(V_L,V_R)$, task-defined values $(V_E,V_S)$, resource state and both choice variables. Ridge regression with $\lambda=5$ was used because the spatially mapped and task-defined value pairs were collinear by construction. Encoding trajectories were projected onto earning/spending and left/right choice axes. Mean encoding strength from 0 to 500~ms after offer onset was compared between the two choice coordinates within each region. Onset and half-peak latencies were likewise compared within each region to test whether the more strongly encoded coordinate emerged earlier. The analyses were repeated for pooled and individual-monkey pseudo-populations (Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_strength}--\ref{tab:supp_choice_t50}). \textbf{[TODO: specify the pairing, inferential unit and multiple-comparison procedure for the strength and latency comparisons.]}

\subsection*{Supplementary Methods}

\subsubsection*{Behavioral-model controls}
\paragraph{Non-parametric optimization.}
Predicted probabilities were clipped to $[10^{-10},1-10^{-10}]$ for numerical stability. Parameters were initialized with small random values; optimization used a maximum of 2,000 iterations and function and step tolerances of $10^{-8}$.

\paragraph{Leave-one-session-out cross-validation.}
To evaluate out-of-sample predictive performance, we applied leave-one-session-out (LOSO) cross-validation separately within each subject. In each fold, model parameters were estimated from the pooled data from all remaining sessions and evaluated on the held-out session. Performance on the held-out session was quantified as the average negative log-likelihood per trial (NLL per trial). The per-fold loss difference was defined as $\Delta L=L_\mathrm{fixed}-L_\mathrm{free}$, such that positive values indicate better generalization of the free-$\alpha$ model. Loss differences were tested against zero across held-out sessions using two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests.

\paragraph{Session-wise $\alpha$ estimation.}
To assess whether the fitted utility curvature was consistently recovered across recording sessions, the free-$\alpha$ model was fitted independently to each session. As in the primary model, $\alpha$ was constrained to $[0,1]$. Estimates were pooled within subject and displayed as probability histograms with a bin width of 0.05; dashed lines mark the subject-specific medians. \textbf{[TODO: Because $\alpha=1$ is the upper boundary of the fitted parameter space, replace or justify the current signed-rank test against $\alpha=1$ before reporting inferential statistics for this analysis.]}

\paragraph{Model predictions.}
Observed and model-predicted earning proportions were calculated for each offer-condition--token-holding combination. Predictions were generated from the subject-level parametric utility model described in the main Methods and plotted using the same offer-condition colors as the observed choice proportions.

\paragraph{Reaction-time analysis.}
Decision difficulty was indexed by $|(\Delta V-b)/\gamma|$, with smaller values indicating proximity to the fitted behavioral indifference point. Within each subject, trials were divided at the median $|(\Delta V-b)/\gamma|$ into hard (below-median) and easy (above-median) groups. Reaction-time distributions are shown separately for the two groups, with vertical lines marking their medians. \textbf{[TODO: Specify the reaction-time start and end events, exclusion criteria, treatment of trials at the median, statistical unit and inferential test. \subsubsection*{Resource-state controls}
For the shuffle control, token-holding-group labels were independently permuted across trials within each unit before repeating unit selection, preferred-group assignment, sign alignment, normalization and peak re-centering. Ten permutations were performed per unit, and their mean selective proportion and peak-aligned curves defined the null estimates. Individual-monkey analyses repeated the complete procedure used for the pooled results (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}).

\subsubsection*{Value-encoding controls}
\paragraph{Population value-space sampling and illustration.}
The value-space analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D,E used the same 50/50 trial split and unit-resampling procedure, with 100 bootstrap iterations, 300 units per iteration for the pooled analysis and 100 units per iteration for each individual monkey. Regression coefficients and TDR axes were re-estimated in every iteration. To select a single readily interpretable value space for visualization in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D, we scored each bootstrap iteration by the geometric mean of its test-set $V_E$ and $V_S$ encoding strengths, each first averaged across the three resource-state groups:
\begin{equation}
S_p=\sqrt{\overline{R^2_{V_E,p}}\,\overline{R^2_{V_S,p}}}.
\end{equation}
The iteration with the highest $S_p$ was displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D. This selection was used only for visualization; the descriptive range summaries in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}E were calculated in every bootstrap iteration and are reported as the mean $\pm$ SD across all 100 iterations.

\paragraph{Geometry metrics and shuffle controls.}
To summarize the scale of the projected geometry along each value axis, the nine coordinates on each axis were sorted within every resource-state group and bootstrap iteration. We defined the trimmed earning-axis and spending-axis ranges as
\begin{align}
R_E &= x_{(8)}-x_{(2)},\\
R_S &= y_{(8)}-y_{(2)},
\end{align}
where $x_{(k)}$ and $y_{(k)}$ denote the $k$th ordered coordinates on the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes, respectively. Excluding the single most extreme coordinate at each end reduced the influence of any one condition mean. Within each resource-state group and bootstrap iteration, the aspect ratio was defined as
\begin{equation}
A=\frac{R_S}{R_E}.
\end{equation}
An aspect ratio greater than 1 indicates that the offer representation extends further along the spending-value axis than along the earning-value axis. Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}E shows the mean $\pm$ SD of $A$ across all 100 bootstrap iterations; the component ranges are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}.

Two shuffle controls were applied to the pooled dataset and repeated the complete regression, cross-validation, projection and geometry procedure. For the value-label shuffle, paired earning--spending offer labels were permuted across trials within each unit and resource-state group, after which $V_E$ and $V_S$ were recomputed from the shuffled offer pair and the original token count. This preserved resource-state composition while disrupting the mapping between offer value and neural activity. For the token-holding-label shuffle, $W$ labels were permuted within each unit and offer condition, after which $V_E$, $V_S$ and the low, middle and high resource-state assignments were recomputed using the shuffled token counts. This preserved offer-condition composition while disrupting the mapping between resource state and neural activity. Each control used 100 bootstrap iterations and the same sampling and best-bootstrap visualization procedures as the unshuffled pooled analysis.

\subsubsection*{Choice-format controls}
The same onset- and half-peak-latency definitions were applied to the earning/spending and left/right choice trajectories in Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:supp_choice_onset} and \ref{tab:supp_choice_t50}. Within each brain area, the two choice coordinates were compared using two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests across bootstrap iterations in the pooled dataset and each individual monkey. Iterations without a valid latency estimate were excluded from the corresponding comparison.
