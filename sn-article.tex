\abstract{Organisms must decide whether to acquire resources for future needs or consume current holdings for immediate benefit; adjusting this trade-off to current holdings is essential for allocating resources efficiently. Whether non-human primates make such resource-state-dependent earning--spending decisions, and how the brain links resource state to value and choice, remain unknown. Here three macaques chose between earning tokens and spending accumulated tokens for water, with token holdings changing continuously through their choices. As holdings increased, monkeys shifted systematically from earning to spending. Behavioral modeling showed that this shift reflected a decrease in the relative value of earning and an increase in the relative value of spending. Prefrontal recordings revealed neural representations spanning the same computational sequence. Anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) encoded current resource state and option values conditional on that state, while ACC and OFC updated resource-state representations after outcomes. OFC carried the most comprehensive option-value and good-based choice signals, whereas DLPFC predominantly represented action-based choice. These findings identify distributed prefrontal computations through which current resource state reshapes value processing and is expressed in earning--spending choice.}

\keywords{Resource state, Earning--spending, Orbitofrontal cortex, Anterior cingulate cortex, Dorsolateral prefrontal cortex}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Animals often face conflicting demands: obtaining more of one resource can come at the expense of satisfying another need \cite{stephens1986foraging,hengeveld2009optimal}. Efficient allocation therefore depends not only on the available outcomes but also on the resources already held. As one resource accumulates, the marginal value of acquiring more declines relative to alternative outcomes, consistent with diminishing marginal utility \cite{bernoulli1954exposition,stephens1986foraging}. In human societies, money makes this problem ubiquitous: because it can be accumulated and exchanged across needs, people repeatedly choose between earning more for future use and spending current holdings for immediate benefit. However, studies in humans alone cannot determine whether this dependence on monetary holdings is a product of human institutions or reflects a more general capacity, nor can they reveal its implementation at the level of individual neurons. Addressing these questions requires an animal model that can accumulate and exchange a symbolic resource while permitting direct measurement of neural activity. Non-human primates (NHPs) meet these requirements: they can acquire, retain and exchange tokens \cite{sousaUseTokensRewards2001,chenHowBasicAre2006a,addessiAreRootsHuman2020,beran2021non,leca2021acquisition,seo2009behavioral}, and their economic preferences change with accumulated token holdings \cite{yangPrimateAnteriorInsular2022c,ferroAccumulationVirtualTokens2025a}. What remains missing is a paradigm that joins two questions previously studied largely apart: how current token holdings affect decisions, and how values are assigned and compared across reward categories. Existing token tasks typically restrict choices to token gains and losses or impose fixed exchange thresholds, rather than placing acquisition of a symbolic resource in direct competition with its consumption for a primary reward while holdings change continuously.

This behavioral problem defines a neural computation linking current resource state to option value and ultimately to choice. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), orbitofrontal cortex (OFC) and dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) have all been implicated in value-based decision-making. ACC activity tracks accumulated token holdings and other wealth-like reference states, making it a candidate for representing and updating resource state \cite{seo2009behavioral,juechemsVentromedialPrefrontalCortex2017,nguyenNeuralRepresentationDecisional2025a}. OFC broadly represents offer values, chosen values and choices between goods \cite{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b,padoa-schioppaOrbitofrontalCortexNeural2017,rudebeckOrbitofrontalCortex2018,ballestaValuesEncodedOrbitofrontal2020a,ballestaOrbitofrontalCortexContributes2022}. DLPFC also carries value-related activity, together with strong spatial and action-related signals \cite{cai2014contributions,lin2021evidence}. These findings characterize the component signals, but not how they operate together when resource state and cross-category valuation must be combined. Specifically, it is unknown how current holdings modulate the values of earning and spending, whether these option values are recoded into the value of the selected option, and how the resulting decision is expressed in good-based and action-based coordinates.

Here we combined a new earning--spending task with computational modeling and single-neuron recordings from ACC, OFC and DLPFC in three rhesus monkeys. Token holdings carried continuously across trials as monkeys chose between acquiring tokens and spending them for water, thereby coupling a changing resource state to cross-category value comparison. As holdings increased, monkeys shifted systematically from earning to spending, and a state-dependent utility model explained this shift through opposing changes in the relative values of the two options. Across prefrontal cortex, neural populations represented current resource state, incorporated it into option-value coding and subsequently represented the value of the selected option before categorical choice signals emerged. ACC and OFC updated resource-state representations after outcomes; OFC carried the most complete option-value and good-based choice signals, whereas DLPFC predominantly represented action-based choice. Together, these results trace a resource--value--choice sequence through which current holdings reshape valuation and are expressed in earning--spending decisions.

\subsection{Resource state shifts earning--spending preferences}

Three rhesus macaques were trained on an earning--spending task (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}A) in which they chose between acquiring virtual tokens and spending accumulated tokens for water rewards. Each trial paired one of three earning options, distinguished by earning rate ($V_\text{Earn}$), with one of four spending options, distinguished by water magnitude ($V_\text{Spend}$) (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}B). Tokens carried over across trials until spent, so accumulated token holdings constituted a continuously evolving resource state. Each choice therefore depended on both the current offers and the resources available at that moment.

The initial choice on each trial revealed two consistent behavioral patterns (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}C). First, earning probability followed the illustrative color ordering of offer conditions: across resource states, monkeys were more likely to earn when the earning rate was faster and the spending reward was smaller. This ordering indicates that all three monkeys distinguished the offer contingencies. Second, within individual offer conditions, earning probability generally declined as tokens accumulated. Thus, choices reflected not only the current offers but also the animal's current resource state.

This state-dependent shift is consistent with diminishing marginal utility: accumulating tokens reduces the marginal benefit of earning an additional token and the opportunity cost of spending one. To assess the change in valuation without imposing a particular functional form, we estimated non-parametric subjective weights for the seven option stimuli at each of the 24 token levels (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}D). In all three monkeys, the subjective weights of earning options decreased, whereas those of spending options increased, as tokens accumulated, directly demonstrating resource-state-dependent valuation.

We next formalized this account using a parametric choice model based on diminishing marginal utility (see Methods). The model integrated current resource state with the offer attributes to estimate earning and spending values. Earning probability was a sigmoid function of the resulting decision variable, $\Delta V=(V_E-V_S-\mathrm{bias})/\gamma$, in all three monkeys (Fig.~\ref{fig:behavior}E and Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}C). This account generalized to held-out sessions and was consistently recovered in session-wise fits across all three subjects (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}A,B). Moreover, the resulting decision variable predicted decision time: responses were slower when earning and spending values were more closely matched (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_behavior_summary}D). Together, these results support a stable resource-state-dependent account of choice and identify $\Delta V$ as a behaviorally relevant decision variable.

\subsection{Distributed prefrontal tuning represents current resource state}
Having established that current resource state systematically shifted earning--spending preferences, we next asked how it was represented by individual prefrontal neurons. We divided holdings into five approximately logarithmically spaced groups ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15] and [16--24] tokens) to accommodate the compressed scaling commonly observed for numerical magnitude \cite{niederMiller2003compressed,niederDehaene2009number}, while yielding more comparable trial counts across groups. An ACC neuron responded most strongly in the highest resource-state group (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}A,B), whereas an OFC neuron showed maximal firing in the intermediate [8--15]-token group (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}C,D). Across ACC, DLPFC and OFC, preferred resource states were distributed across all five groups (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}E).

We next asked how these single-neuron tuning functions were organized at the population level. Neurons preferring different resource-state groups exhibited similarly shaped tuning curves centered at different points along the resource-state axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}F--H). Re-centering the curves on their preferred group revealed a common profile in all three regions, with firing rates declining progressively as resource state moved away from the preferred level (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}I--K). This graded decline disappeared when resource-state-group labels were shuffled and the complete analysis was repeated, indicating that it did not arise solely from selecting and aligning response peaks (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}). Together, these heterogeneous tuning profiles provided a distributed population representation capable of distinguishing different levels of the animal's current resource state.

\subsection{Temporal dynamics and choice-dependent updating of resource-state representations}
The preceding analyses established that prefrontal populations represented current resource state, but did not reveal how this representation evolved across task epochs. We therefore applied cross-temporal decoding based on targeted dimensionality reduction (TDR) \cite{mante2013context}: at each time bin, a TDR-derived resource-state axis was fitted independently and tested at every other bin, yielding a $24 \times 24$ matrix of decoding accuracy ($R^2$) spanning the offer-onset and wallet-update epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}A). Resource state was decodable even before offer onset in all three regions, consistent with the wallet remaining visible throughout the ITI. During the offer-onset epoch, significant decoding was concentrated near the temporal diagonal, demonstrating that the resource-state representation evolved dynamically over time. By contrast, decoding generalized broadly across the wallet-update epoch, demonstrating a stable resource-state representation during this period. Within the offer-onset epoch, resource-state decoding was significantly stronger in ACC than in DLPFC ($p=6.6\times10^{-34}$) or OFC ($p=7.2\times10^{-34}$). \textbf{[TODO: specify the observation unit, pairing across regions, matrix entries included, test direction and correction for the two reported cross-region comparisons.] }Thus, resource-state representations were dynamic during the offer-onset epoch but stable during the wallet-update epoch, with ACC showing the strongest resource-state decoding during offer evaluation. Individual-animal analyses are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_cross_temporal}.

Trial averaging in the cross-temporal analysis could obscure the transition structure associated with each wallet update. We therefore projected population activity from $-100$ to $200$~ms relative to wallet update onto TDR-derived choice and resource-state axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}B). Earning drove activity toward the higher-resource-state direction in ACC and OFC, whereas spending drove it toward the lower-resource-state direction. To quantify these shifts, we regressed the resource-state-axis projection against time separately for earning and spending trials within each bootstrap iteration; the resulting temporal coefficient, $\beta_{\mathrm{update}}$, measured the direction and rate of movement along the resource-state axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics}C). We defined choice-dependent resource-state updating as the joint presence of a significantly positive earning coefficient and a significantly negative spending coefficient. ACC and OFC satisfied this bidirectional criterion, whereas DLPFC did not in the pooled analysis. This pattern was broadly reproduced in individual animals, and the bidirectional effect was absent after shuffling either choice or resource-state labels (Supplementary Table~\ref{tab:supp_state_updating}). Thus, ACC and OFC not only represented current resource state but also updated that representation when the wallet changed, providing an updated state signal that could influence subsequent value coding.

\subsection{Resource state modulates prefrontal offer-value representations}
Having established that prefrontal populations represented and updated the current resource state, we next asked whether this state was incorporated into the neural processing of earning and spending values. As established by the behavioral model, diminishing marginal utility caused earning values to decrease and converge as tokens accumulated, whereas spending values increased while retaining the separation attributable to reward magnitude (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}A,B; see Methods). Representative OFC neurons exhibited response patterns consistent with these model predictions (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}A,B). We quantified these relationships indirectly at the single-unit level by testing whether the signs of state-independent offer-value and resource-state coefficients were systematically associated. The predicted same-sign association for spending value was significant in all three regions, whereas the predicted opposite-sign association for earning value was not (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_single_unit}C). The absence of an earning-value association may reflect the smaller number of neurons encoding earning value and the consequently limited sensitivity of this sign-based analysis.

We therefore tested resource-dependent earning value directly using a nested-model comparison. Relative to a baseline model ($M_1$), a second model ($M_2$) additionally included the resource-state-dependent earning value $V_E$ derived from the behavioral model (see Methods). Around wallet update, the increase in explained variance, $\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}$, was significantly greater than the value-label-shuffled control in ACC, DLPFC and OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}C). This result shows that neural activity tracked the interaction between earning value and current resource state, rather than their independent effects alone. An analogous nested-model test was not identifiable for $V_S$ because its state-dependent component was additive with resource state and was therefore absorbed by the resource-state terms already present in $M_1$ (see Methods).

We next used TDR to examine how this state dependence was expressed in a population value space defined by earning- and spending-value axes (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D). We quantified its geometry using the aspect ratio between the spending- and earning-axis ranges. Because the behavioral model predicted that earning values would converge while the separation among spending values would be preserved, this ratio was expected to increase with resource state. The aspect ratio increased monotonically in ACC and showed a weaker, non-monotonic tendency to be higher at high than at low resource states in DLPFC and OFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:value_modulation}E). Resource-state-label shuffling attenuated this state-related structure, whereas value-label shuffling collapsed the underlying value-space geometry (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}), supporting the presence of a modest resource-state dependence in the population representation. Together, the joint-encoding analysis, nested-model comparison and population geometry show that current resource state modulated prefrontal representations of both earning and spending opportunities during state updating.

\subsection{Prefrontal encoding of chosen value during resource-state-dependent decisions}
Having established that resource state reshaped the neural representation of the available options, we next asked how these state-dependent option values were transformed into a representation of the selected option. The representative unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}A,B illustrates how value tuning depended on the eventual choice. Its firing rate increased with $V_E$ under both choice conditions, but the slope was steeper during earning choices (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}A). For $V_S$, the tuning slope was positive during spending choices but negative during earning choices (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}B). Thus, the neuron's value sensitivity was stronger---and, for $V_S$, directionally aligned---when the corresponding option was selected, a response pattern consistent with chosen-value encoding.

We then tested chosen-value encoding at the population level using choice-conditioned TDR. During earning choices, population activity encoded $V_E$ more strongly than $V_S$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}C,D); during spending choices, this relationship reversed, with stronger encoding of $V_S$ than $V_E$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}E,F). We quantified this reversal using the interaction contrast $D=[R^2(V_E\mid\mathrm{Earn})-R^2(V_S\mid\mathrm{Earn})]-[R^2(V_E\mid\mathrm{Spend})-R^2(V_S\mid\mathrm{Spend})]$. The contrast was significantly positive in \textbf{[TODO: specify the supported regions]} during the predefined \textbf{[TODO: specify time window]} (ACC, $D=[EFFECT]$, $[STAT]=[VALUE]$, $p=[P]$; DLPFC, $D=[EFFECT]$, $[STAT]=[VALUE]$, $p=[P]$; OFC, $D=[EFFECT]$, $[STAT]=[VALUE]$, $p=[P]$). Thus, the identity of the more strongly represented value depended systematically on which option was selected, providing population-level evidence for chosen-value encoding.

To obtain a single trial-wise measure for temporal analyses, we used signed chosen advantage, $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}=(V_E-V_S-b)c$, as an operational measure of chosen value, where $c=+1$ on earning choices and $c=-1$ on spending choices. The fitted bias $b$ shifts the indifference point but cannot be uniquely assigned to either option value; this measure therefore captures the chosen option's advantage over the unchosen option rather than its absolute value. We compared the temporal encoding profiles of $V_E$, $V_S$, $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ and categorical choice (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}G--I). Chosen-value-related activity was represented in ACC, DLPFC and OFC, alongside regionally varying representations of the individual earning and spending values. OFC carried significant $V_E$, $V_S$ and $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ signals, whereas ACC and DLPFC showed more asymmetric value-encoding profiles (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_value_summary}).

To compare the timing of value and choice representations, we calculated the half-peak latency, $t_{50}$, defined as the first time point between offer onset and the response peak at which encoding exceeded the midpoint between the pre-offer baseline and peak $R^2$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}J--L). In all three regions, $V_E$, $V_S$ and $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ reached $t_{50}$ significantly earlier than the categorical choice signal (pairwise two-sided Wilcoxon rank-sum tests; Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}J--L). A complementary onset-latency analysis yielded the same temporal ordering (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_value_latency}). The earlier rise of $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ argues against the possibility that it merely reflects a categorical choice already represented in population activity and is consistent with its role as an intermediate variable linking value processing to the emerging choice signal.

Finally, we asked whether the behaviorally relevant value difference $\Delta V=V_E-V_S$ constituted a dominant encoding feature at the single-unit level. Because $\Delta V$ is an exact linear combination of $V_E$ and $V_S$, linear regression cannot distinguish a unit genuinely encoding the difference from a unit with mixed selectivity for the two option values. A joint-encoding analysis found no significant enrichment of the coefficient-sign combinations expected for an explicit value-difference representation in ACC, DLPFC or OFC (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_delta_value}; ACC, $p=[P_{\mathrm{ACC}}]$; DLPFC, $p=[P_{\mathrm{DLPFC}}]$; OFC, $p=[P_{\mathrm{OFC}}]$). Thus, although $\Delta V$ captured behavior, it was not a dominant encoding feature at the single-unit level.

Together, these results identify chosen value as a prominent downstream prefrontal representation during resource-state-dependent earning--spending decisions. Both single-neuron responses and population activity preferentially represented the value of the selected option, and this conclusion was robust to individual-animal, trial-count-matching and label-shuffle controls (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_choice_conditioned}). Moreover, chosen-value-related signals reached half-peak encoding before the categorical choice signal, consistent with chosen value serving as an intermediate variable between resource-state-dependent option-value processing and choice.

\subsection{Regional organization of good-based and action-based choice signals}
The selected option in this task specified both an economic outcome---earning or spending---and the spatial action used to implement it. We therefore asked how this downstream choice representation was organized across good-based and action-based formats in prefrontal cortex. The representative DLPFC unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}A primarily distinguished leftward from rightward responses, whereas the representative OFC unit in Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}B primarily distinguished earning from spending choices. Across the recorded units, ACC and OFC were biased toward good-based selectivity, whereas DLPFC was biased toward action-based selectivity, although mixed selectivity was present in all three regions (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_choice_units}).

We next characterized this organization at the population level using TDR to extract separate earning/spending and left/right choice axes while accounting for option values and current resource state. Following offer onset, the four choice conditions separated primarily along the earning/spending axis in ACC and OFC, but primarily along the left/right axis in DLPFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}C). Time-resolved encoding strength confirmed the same regional pattern: good-based choice encoding was stronger than action-based encoding in ACC and OFC, whereas action-based encoding dominated in DLPFC (Fig.~\ref{fig:choice_representation}D). Thus, both choice formats were detectable across prefrontal cortex, but their relative expression differed across regions.

This regional organization raised the question of whether the dominant OFC good-based signal preceded the dominant DLPFC action-based signal, as predicted by a strictly serial good-to-action transformation. In the pooled analysis, good-based choice encoding emerged earlier than action-based encoding within OFC, whereas DLPFC showed the complementary pattern, with earlier action-based than good-based encoding; the latter difference was also evident in monkey T. Critically, direct comparison of OFC good-based and DLPFC action-based onset latencies revealed no consistent temporal ordering across the pooled and individual-animal analyses (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_choice_latency}). The dominant signals in the two regions therefore emerged on similar timescales, arguing against a simple serial cascade in which an OFC choice is completed before being relayed to DLPFC for action mapping.

Together, these results show how the output of resource-state-dependent value processing was expressed in regionally preferred reference frames: ACC and OFC predominantly represented the identity of the selected good, whereas DLPFC predominantly represented the spatial action used to implement the choice. The absence of a stable temporal ordering between the dominant OFC good-based and DLPFC action-based signals argues against a strictly serial cortical transformation and instead suggests that the two choice formats emerge through distributed prefrontal processing.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec3}

The earning--spending task allowed a symbolic resource to accumulate and be consumed while macaques repeatedly chose between acquiring more of that resource and exchanging it for an immediate reward. Across animals, current token holdings systematically shifted choice, and prefrontal activity represented both the current holdings and the values of the available actions conditional on those holdings. Resource-state representations were updated after earning and spending outcomes in ACC and OFC, while state-dependent value signals and choice signals were distributed across ACC, DLPFC and OFC with different regional emphases. These findings support a computational account in which an evolving resource state is not merely contextual information carried alongside a decision, but a variable incorporated into the valuation process that guides earning--spending choice.

The behavioral effect extends previous token paradigms by placing acquisition and expenditure in direct competition without a fixed exchange threshold. As holdings accumulated, the relative preference for spending increased, the non-parametric option weights shifted in opposite directions for earning and spending, and the marginal-utility model captured both choice and decision-time patterns. These observations are consistent with diminishing marginal utility, but do not establish it as the unique source of the behavioral effect. Token holdings also covaried with recent earning and spending history and, over longer timescales, with session progress and cumulative water intake. The model treated water value and the cost of sustained fixation as stationary or simple functions; unmodeled satiety or effort dynamics could therefore contribute to the estimated state dependence. Orthogonal manipulation of token holdings and motivational state will be required to determine how much of the behavioral effect is specifically attributable to the utility of the accumulated resource.

The neural data identify complementary properties of the resource-state representation. Neurons preferred different resource-state levels, and their heterogeneous tuning profiles formed a distributed population code capable of distinguishing the current state. Around wallet update, ACC and OFC populations moved in opposite directions along the resource-state axis following earning and spending, as expected for an increment and decrement of the represented state. The wallet, however, remained continuously visible, and all tokens had the same denomination. Consequently, total resource value, displayed token numerosity and associated visual features were not dissociated. The present results therefore establish a task-relevant representation of current token holdings, but not a representation that is necessarily abstract or internally maintained. Concealing the wallet and independently varying token number and total value would test whether the same neural code tracks an internal resource state across changes in sensory format.

The principal mechanistic result is that current resource state was incorporated into prefrontal value-related activity. A model containing state-dependent earning value explained activity beyond state-independent offer values and an arbitrary main effect of resource state, and population value geometry changed with holdings in the direction predicted by behavior. The effect was therefore not reducible to separate representations of token holdings and state-invariant offer values: the relative neural representation of earning and spending opportunities changed with the animal's current holdings.

This computation was distributed but regionally differentiated. ACC showed the strongest resource-state decoding during offer evaluation and, together with OFC, bidirectional updating after outcomes. OFC carried the most complete set of resource-state, earning-value, spending-value and chosen-value signals, consistent with established accounts of OFC offer- and chosen-value coding \citep{padoa-schioppaNeuronsOrbitofrontalCortex2006b, padoa-schioppaOrbitofrontalCortexNeural2017}. DLPFC showed weaker and more asymmetric value signals and predominantly represented the action used to report the choice. This pattern makes OFC a candidate locus---or recipient---of resource-state-dependent value integration, rather than establishing it as a unique integrative hub.

The resource-state modulation was expressed prominently around wallet update, when the represented state itself changed. The present data do not determine whether this integration occurs locally within OFC, is inherited from ACC or another region, or emerges through interactions among these areas.

The organization of choice signals further constrains the circuit account. OFC preferentially represented the selected good, whereas DLPFC preferentially represented the spatial action, consistent with their established representational biases \citep{cai2014contributions, lin2021evidence}. Yet both regions also carried signals in their non-preferred reference frame, arguing against completely segregated modules. Moreover, the dominant good-based signal in OFC did not consistently precede the dominant action-based signal in DLPFC. This observation is inconsistent with a simple feedforward cascade in which a completed OFC choice is invariably transmitted to DLPFC for action mapping. It does not, however, exclude serial interactions that vary across trials or neuronal subpopulations and are obscured in pseudo-population onset estimates. The data are therefore most consistent with partially specialized, interacting computations in which good-based and action-based choice formats develop on overlapping timescales.

The circuit interpretation is also constrained by the recordings and task design. The experiment cannot determine whether neural tuning reflects absolute holdings or deviation from an internally adapted reference point. Recordings were combined into pseudo-populations, precluding trial-by-trial estimates of cross-region coupling or information flow. Moreover, all evidence is correlational: resource-state and value signals may participate in the decision, but their necessity has not been demonstrated.

A decisive next experiment would transiently conceal the wallet, dissociate token numerosity from total resource value, introduce unexpected state transitions and record simultaneously across regions. Temporally targeted perturbations during state presentation, updating and offer evaluation could then distinguish state representation from value integration and test their causal contributions to behavior.

More broadly, value-guided behavior depends on the state of the organism and on the resources available for future action. The present results extend this principle to an accumulated, exchangeable symbolic resource: prefrontal value representations changed with the animal's evolving holdings rather than remaining fixed properties of the offers. Resource-state-dependent economic choice can therefore be understood as an instance of a more general neural computation in which current state reshapes valuation and is expressed through regionally differentiated decision and action representations.

\section{Methods}\label{sec4}

\subsection{Subjects}
Three male rhesus monkeys (\textit{Macaca mulatta}; monkeys W, U and T) were used. Their body weights during the experiments were 6.3--8.0, 3.5--4.6 and 5.8--6.7~kg, respectively. All procedures were approved by the relevant institutional animal-care committee. \textbf{[TODO: provide the committee's current official English name and protocol number.]}

\subsection{Experimental apparatus}
Experiments were conducted in a sound-attenuated, dimly lit room maintained at 20--25$^\circ$C. Monkeys sat in a custom primate chair with the head fixed and viewed a 23.6-inch monitor at a distance of approximately 60~cm. Visual stimulus presentation and reward delivery were controlled using MonkeyLogic~2 running in MATLAB. Eye position was sampled at 1,000~Hz using an EyeLink~1000 infrared eye tracker. Water was delivered through a computer-controlled solenoid valve calibrated at approximately 0.2~$\mu$l~ms$^{-1}$.

\subsection{Behavioral task}
Monkeys performed an earning--spending task (Figs.~\ref{fig:behavior}A,~B) in which tokens accumulated across trials and could subsequently be spent for water. Current holdings were displayed by a central ``wallet'', a $5\times5$ circle array approximately $4.3^\circ$ wide. Filled circles indicated the number of tokens currently held. The wallet remained visible during the inter-trial interval (ITI), when the option stimuli were absent, and updated immediately after each successful earning or spending event. Holdings were bounded between 0 and 25 tokens. Earning at 25 tokens did not add another token, and spending at 0 tokens did not remove one, although the attempted choice was recorded. Each session began at 0 tokens.

Each trial presented one earning stimulus and one spending stimulus. The two stimuli appeared at $6^\circ$ eccentricity, at locations rotated $90^\circ$ clockwise and counter-clockwise from the monkey's gaze direction at trial onset. Assignment of earning and spending stimuli to the two locations was randomized across trials. The earning rate and spending reward were sampled independently with equal probability, yielding 12 offer conditions. The three earning rates were one token per 250, 200 or 100~ms of continuous fixation (4, 5 or 10 tokens~s$^{-1}$). Spending always removed one token per 200~ms (5 tokens~s$^{-1}$) and delivered the reward associated with the spending stimulus at each removal. For monkeys W and U, solenoid-open durations were 0, 20, 35 and 55~ms per token; for monkey T, they were 15, 25, 35 and 70~ms per token.

Monkeys selected an option by directing gaze to it and maintaining fixation. Continued fixation could generate multiple token updates, and monkeys could switch between the two options before trial end. Trial duration was drawn from an exponential distribution with a mean of 3,000~ms and truncated to 1,000--5,000~ms; the ITI was approximately 1~s. We define \emph{offer onset} as the simultaneous appearance of the two option stimuli, an \emph{initial choice} as the first option fixation that successfully produced an earning or spending event, a \emph{wallet update} as an individual token addition or deduction, and a \emph{choice switch} as a transition to the other option in which both the preceding and subsequent fixations produced a token event and the inter-fixation interval did not exceed 150~ms.

\subsection{Training}
Following acquisition of a standard delayed saccade task, monkeys were progressively trained on the earning--spending task. Reward magnitude ($V_\text{Spend}$) was initially set high to ensure adequate daily water intake, then gradually reduced as each monkey's choice behavior converged to a stable pattern. Full training was completed in approximately 6 months for all subjects.

\subsection{Surgery and recording-site localization}
Before behavioral training, each monkey underwent surgery to implant a headpost for head fixation. After behavior stabilized, an acrylic recording chamber was implanted over prefrontal cortex. The chamber's inner dimensions were 25~mm along the anterior--posterior axis, 20~mm along the medial--lateral axis and 10~mm along the superior--inferior axis. Chambers were placed over the left hemisphere in monkeys W and T and the right hemisphere in monkey U. Bone within the chamber was removed while leaving the dura intact. All surgeries were conducted under aseptic conditions. Anesthesia was induced with ketamine hydrochloride (5--15~mg~kg$^{-1}$, i.m.) and maintained with 1.5--2\% isoflurane; heart rate, blood pressure, body temperature and end-tidal CO$_2$ were monitored continuously.

After chamber implantation, structural MRI was acquired using a Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma 3-T scanner. A 1~mol~l$^{-1}$ manganese chloride tetrahydrate stock solution was diluted 66-fold and placed in blind holes of the recording grid as a contrast marker. Grid holes were 1~mm in diameter, spaced 2~mm apart and aligned with the electrode-penetration axis. Recording locations were assigned using the spatial correspondence between the contrast-filled grid and brain anatomy together with physiological landmarks encountered during penetrations, including changes in spiking density and impedance. ACC recordings corresponded approximately to BA~24, OFC to BA~11 and DLPFC to BA~46; their locations and anterior--posterior extents are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_recording_sites}.

\subsection{Neurophysiology}
Neuronal activity was recorded using 16-channel Plexon S-Probes (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX, USA). Adjacent recording contacts were separated by 100~$\mu$m, such that the 16 contacts spanned 1.5~mm along the probe shaft. Probes were advanced to the target depth using an EPS multichannel microdrive (Alpha Omega Engineering, Nof HaGalil, Israel). Signals were acquired using an AlphaLab SnR system (Alpha Omega Engineering) at 44~kHz and band-pass filtered online between 300 and 6,000~Hz. Electrophysiological data were collected over 52 recording sessions in monkey W, 63 in monkey U and 64 in monkey T (179 sessions in total). In total, we recorded 679 units from ACC (247 from monkey W and 432 from T; no ACC recordings were obtained from U), 1,056 from DLPFC (127, 491 and 438 from W, U and T) and 903 from OFC (111, 446 and 346 from W, U and T; Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_recording_sites}).

\subsection{Behavioral analysis and computational modeling}

All behavioral sessions in which electrophysiological data were recorded were included. Throughout the Methods, \emph{token holdings} denotes the number of tokens displayed in the wallet at the task event specified for each analysis and is represented by $W$ in equations. Token holdings operationalized the animal's current resource state; the term \emph{wealth-like} is reserved for the broader interpretation of this task variable. Behavioral analyses used only the initial successful choice on each trial, thereby avoiding dependence introduced by repeated within-trial selections. Trials were excluded if the initial fixation did not produce an earning or spending event. Observations at the wallet boundaries (0 or 25 tokens) were also excluded because one option could not change the resource state. Consequently, all behavioral value models were evaluated over holdings of 1--24 tokens.

\subsubsection{Non-parametric weight estimation}
To quantify the modulation of option value by token holdings without assuming a specific functional form, we estimated subjective weights for the seven shapes (three earning, four spending) at each of the 24 token-holding levels. Earning probability was modeled by a sigmoid function:
\begin{equation}
P_\text{earn} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp\!\bigl(-(V_{e,w} - V_{s,w})\bigr)},
\end{equation}
where $V_{e,w}$ and $V_{s,w}$ are the estimated weights of the earning and spending options at token-holding level $w$. To regularize the solution and encourage gradual variation across adjacent levels, we augmented the cross-entropy loss with an $L_2$ smoothing penalty:
\begin{align}
L = & -\sum_{i} \bigl[c_i \ln \hat{p}_i + (1 - c_i) \ln(1 - \hat{p}_i)\bigr] \\
& + \lambda \sum_{e,s,w} \bigl(V_{e/s,w} - V_{e/s,w+1}\bigr)^2,
\end{align}
where $c_i \in \{0,1\}$ is the binary choice (1 = earn, 0 = spend), $\hat{p}_i$ is the predicted earning probability, and $\lambda = 10$ controls the smoothing strength. Parameters were estimated by unconstrained optimization (\texttt{fminunc}, quasi-Newton algorithm, MATLAB).

Predicted probabilities were clipped to $[10^{-10},1-10^{-10}]$ for numerical stability. Parameters were initialized with small random values; optimization used a maximum of 2,000 iterations and function and step tolerances of $10^{-8}$. Because the likelihood depends only on differences between earning and spending weights, their common level is not identifiable without a reference convention. \textbf{[TODO: specify from the analysis code whether one option was fixed as the reference or the seven weights were centered within each token-holding level.]}

\subsubsection{Parametric utility model}
We developed a parametric model based on marginal utility theory. We assumed a power-law utility function $U(w) = w^\alpha$, where $w$ denotes current holdings and $\alpha$ is the curvature exponent. Reward magnitude was expressed in dimensionless units by assigning the second-smallest magnitude a value of 1 and scaling the remaining magnitudes proportionally. Overall reward scaling was absorbed by the fitted token--reward exchange-rate parameter $ER$. The subjective values of earning and spending were defined as:
\begin{align}
V_\text{Earn} &= \frac{ES}{ER} \cdot \alpha w^{\alpha-1}, \\
V_\text{Spend} &= R \cdot SS - \frac{SS}{ER} \cdot \alpha w^{\alpha-1},
\end{align}
where $ES$ is the earning speed (tokens/s), $R$ is the normalized reward magnitude (in arbitrary units), $ER$ is the exchange rate (tokens per unit reward), and $SS = 5$ tokens/s is the spend speed. The first term in $V_\text{Spend}$ represents the immediate reward benefit; the second captures the opportunity cost of spending a token scaled by its marginal utility. For the behavioral analyses, we defined the normalized decision variable as $\Delta V=(V_\text{Earn}-V_\text{Spend}-\text{bias})/\gamma$, and choice probability was given by:
\begin{equation}
P_\text{earn} = \frac{1}{1 + \exp(-\Delta V)},
\end{equation}
where $\gamma > 0$ is the choice-sensitivity parameter and $\text{bias}$ is a decision bias. Neural analyses of value-difference encoding used the equivalent unnormalized difference $V_E-V_S$; subtracting a constant bias and applying a positive scale factor do not alter the corresponding regression inference because these transformations are absorbed by the intercept and coefficient magnitude, respectively. Free parameters $(\alpha,\, ER,\, \text{bias},\, \gamma)$ were estimated by minimizing cross-entropy loss using \texttt{fmincon} (SQP algorithm, MATLAB) with MultiStart (10 random initializations). Initial exploratory fits allowed $\alpha\geq0$; because all estimates were below 1, subsequent fits constrained $\alpha$ to $[0,1]$ for numerical stability. The marginal-utility expression was evaluated only for $w=1,\ldots,24$; observations at $w=0$ were excluded as described above.

The \textit{fixed-$\alpha$} model served as a nested baseline in which $\alpha$ was constrained to 1, corresponding to linear marginal utility. All remaining parameters were estimated freely, allowing direct assessment of whether token-holding-dependent curvature in utility is necessary to account for the observed choice patterns. Cross-validation, session-wise parameter estimation, model-prediction visualization and reaction-time analyses are described in Supplementary Methods.

\subsection{Neural data preprocessing}

\subsubsection{Spike sorting and active-epoch selection}
Spike sorting was performed automatically using Kilosort4. A subset of automatically sorted units was compared with online sorting and showed concordant active periods, waveforms and firing rates; no additional manual curation was applied. To exclude silent periods caused by electrode drift, loss of isolation or cell injury, firing rate was evaluated in non-overlapping 60-s windows. For each unit, only the longest continuous sequence of windows with a mean firing rate above 1~Hz was retained. The resulting active-epoch boundaries were rounded inward to trial boundaries, such that only complete trials lying within the active epoch were included; no temporal padding was applied.

\subsubsection{Firing-rate estimation and representative-neuron PSTHs}
Spike trains were initially converted to firing rates in 10-ms bins. Analyses based on a single mean firing rate within a predefined window used unsmoothed rates. PSTHs and other time-resolved trajectories were smoothed with a causal Gaussian kernel ($\sigma = 100$~ms; half-window = 300~ms). The kernel spanned only $[-300,0]$~ms relative to each time point, so the smoothed rate depended exclusively on preceding spikes.

\subsection{Targeted dimensionality reduction}
We employed TDR to project population firing patterns into a low-dimensional subspace aligned to task-relevant variables.

\subsubsection{Unit filtering}
Neurons were filtered according to each analysis. For the primary TDR analyses, a unit was retained if it contributed at least 50 valid trials in total and at least 10 valid trials to every required condition group.

\subsubsection{Regression models}
Firing rates at each time bin were z-scored independently: the mean and standard deviation across training trials were computed at each time bin and applied to normalize both training and test responses, preventing test-set information from entering the fitted model. Task variables were likewise z-scored within the training partition. The regression target for each neuron was the z-scored firing rate averaged over the analysis-specific regression window, yielding one scalar observation per trial. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression was then applied to estimate each neuron's coefficient vector $\boldsymbol{\beta}_n \in \mathbb{R}^{V}$; these were stacked row-wise to form the population coefficient matrix $\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times V}$.

For the results in Figures~\ref{fig:choice_representation}, we used the following model (regression window: 0--500~ms aligned to offer onset; projection window: -200--500~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \sum_v \beta_v V_v + \beta_{W}\,W + \sum_c \beta_c Choice_c + \beta_0
\end{equation}
where $V_v \in \{V_L, V_R, V_E, V_S\}$ denote the values of the spatially-mapped and task-defined options, $W$ is the token count at offer onset, and $Choice_c \in \{Choice_{LR}, Choice_{ES}\}$ represents the monkey's choices in the action and good-based domains. Because $V_L$/$V_R$ and $V_E$/$V_S$ are collinear by construction, ridge regression ($\lambda = 5$) replaced OLS for this model to stabilize coefficient estimation; all other models used standard OLS.

For the results in Figure~\ref{fig:resource_state_dynamics} (regression window: $-100$--200~ms relative to token update; projection window: $-100$--200~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1\,V_E + \beta_2\,V_S + \beta_3\,Choice_{ES} + \beta_4\,W
\end{equation}

For the population value-space analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D,E, firing rates were averaged from $-200$ to $300$~ms relative to resource-state update and regressed using the same window:
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_C\,Choice + \beta_W\,W + \beta_E\,V_E + \beta_S\,V_S.
\end{equation}
Here $Choice$ denotes earning versus spending, $W$ is the continuous token count at resource-state update, and $V_E$ and $V_S$ are the resource-state-dependent earning and spending values derived from the free-$\alpha$ behavioral model. The regression coefficient matrix and subsequent QR decomposition used the common column order $[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{Choice},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{W},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_E},\,\boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_S}]$. The third and fourth columns of the resulting orthonormal basis defined the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D.

To isolate value encoding from choice-related activity (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}C--F), trials were partitioned by the monkey's behavioral output (earning vs.\ spending) and independent TDR models were fit within each condition (regression window: 0--500~ms post-offer; projection window: $-200$--500~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1\,V_E + \beta_2\,V_S + \beta_3\,W
\end{equation}
The coefficient matrix was orthogonalized via QR decomposition with column ordering $[\boldsymbol{\beta}_{W},\, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_E},\, \boldsymbol{\beta}_{V_S}]$, yielding encoding axes for $V_E$ and $V_S$ residualized against token-holding effects. Test trials were stratified into tertiles of the corresponding value variable (100 trials per tertile). The analysis was repeated over 100 bootstrap iterations, each resampling units with replacement (100 per individual subject; 300 for the pooled analysis).

To compare the temporal emergence of value and choice signals (Fig.~\ref{fig:chosen_value}G--I), a joint model was fit over the offer-onset epoch (regression window: 0--500~ms; projection window: $-200$--500~ms):
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1\,W + \beta_2\,Choice + \beta_3\,V_E + \beta_4\,V_S + \beta_5\,V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}
\end{equation}
Four separate encoding axes were extracted by repeating QR decomposition with the target variable placed last in the column ordering, ensuring each axis is orthogonal to all others. Test trials were stratified into two groups for the choice axis (150 trials per group) or three tertile groups for value axes (100 trials per group).

\subsubsection{Ordered QR orthonormalization and projection}
The coefficient matrix $\mathbf{B}$ was orthonormalized via QR decomposition:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{Q}\mathbf{R}
\end{equation}
where the columns of $\mathbf{Q} \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times V}$ form an orthonormal basis of task-related population axes. QR decomposition was treated as an ordered variance partition: earlier variables occupied shared coefficient space first, and each later axis represented the component remaining after the preceding variables. For target-specific encoding analyses, QR decomposition was repeated with the target variable placed last, so that the target axis was residualized against all other regressors. For the joint value-space analysis, a single ordered basis $[Choice,W,V_E,V_S]$ was retained. Thus, the $V_E$ axis was residualized against choice and token holdings, whereas the $V_S$ axis was additionally residualized against $V_E$. These two axes define a common ordered value space and were not treated as exchange-symmetric estimates for an unconditional comparison of the absolute magnitudes of $V_E$ and $V_S$ encoding. Time-resolved population signals were obtained by projecting the z-scored test-set firing-rate matrix $\mathbf{X}(t)$ onto the basis:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}(t) = \mathbf{Q}^T \mathbf{X}(t)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Cross-validation and resampling}
To obtain stable estimates of encoding strength and characterize uncertainty, the regression--projection pipeline was repeated over $N_{CV}$ iterations (10 for cross-temporal decoding and 100 for other TDR analyses). In each iteration, units were sampled without replacement (100 per individual monkey; 300 for pooled pseudo-populations), and a fresh 50/50 trial partition was drawn independently for each unit. The training set was used to fit $\mathbf{B}_p$ and derive $\mathbf{Q}_p$; test-set firing rates were then projected onto $\mathbf{Q}_p$. Test trials were assigned to condition groups and resampled with replacement. A fixed total of 300 trials was drawn, distributed evenly across groups, and pooled with the corresponding labels. Encoding strength at each time point was the squared Pearson correlation between projection and group label:
\begin{equation}
R^2(t) = \left[\mathrm{corr}\!\left(\mathbf{p}(t),\, \mathbf{l}\right)\right]^2
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{p}(t)$ is the vector of pooled projections at time $t$ and $\mathbf{l}$ the corresponding group label vector. The mean and standard deviation of $R^2(t)$ across $N_{CV}$ iterations are reported throughout.

Resampling iterations quantify the stability of pseudo-population estimates and are not independent animals or recording sessions. Because the appropriate inferential unit differs among analyses, each test below specifies its sampling unit, pairing and null construction. \textbf{[TODO: for tests currently performed directly across resampling iterations, specify the intended empirical-resampling interpretation and confirm any correction for multiple comparisons.]}

The value-space analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D,E used the same 50/50 trial split and unit-resampling procedure, with 100 bootstrap iterations, 300 units per iteration for the pooled analysis and 100 units per iteration for each individual monkey. Regression coefficients and TDR axes were re-estimated in every iteration. To select a single readily interpretable value space for visualization in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D, we scored each bootstrap iteration by the geometric mean of its test-set $V_E$ and $V_S$ encoding strengths, each first averaged across the three resource-state groups:
\begin{equation}
S_p=\sqrt{\overline{R^2_{V_E,p}}\,\overline{R^2_{V_S,p}}}.
\end{equation}
The iteration with the highest $S_p$ was displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D. This selection was used only for visualization; the descriptive range summaries in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}E were calculated in every bootstrap iteration and are reported as the mean $\pm$ SD across all 100 iterations.

\subsubsection{Cross-temporal decoding framework}
To assess the temporal stability of encoded variables across task epochs, we performed cross-temporal decoding for both token holdings and the value variables ($V_E$, $V_S$, $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$). Time bins were defined at 50~ms resolution. For token holdings, 14 bins spanned the offer-onset epoch ($-200$ to $500$~ms) and 10 bins spanned the wallet-update epoch ($-200$ to $300$~ms), yielding a $24 \times 24$ decoding matrix; for the value variables, 10 bins spanned the offer-onset epoch ($0$ to $500$~ms) and 10 bins the peri-choice epoch ($-200$ to $300$~ms), yielding a $20 \times 20$ matrix. At each training bin, a TDR axis was fitted following the regression and QR orthogonalization procedures described above and used to decode the target variable at every other bin, with $R^2$ computed from tertile-stratified test trials. The procedure was repeated for $N_{CV} = 10$ bootstrap iterations; the reduced number of iterations reflects the computational cost of the full cross-temporal design. In each iteration, units were resampled without replacement (100 per individual subject; 300 for the pooled analysis) and a fresh trial partition was drawn independently per unit. The mean $R^2$ across iterations is reported.

Each matrix entry was tested against a shuffle control in which variable labels were permuted across trials within each unit. Statistical significance was assessed using a one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test comparing real and shuffle $R^2$ values across bootstrap iterations. Entries not significantly exceeding the shuffle distribution are displayed in black.

\subsection{Resource-state representation and updating}
\subsubsection{Single-unit resource-state selectivity and tuning}
To quantify token-holding selectivity, we regressed the mean firing rate in a window of $-300$ to $300$~ms relative to offer onset against task variables:
\begin{equation}
    FR \sim \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 Choice_{ES} + \beta_4 W + \beta_0
    \label{eq:cat_wealth}
\end{equation}
where $W$ denotes the token count at offer onset, treated as a categorical variable with five levels ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15], [16--24]). A logarithmic binning scheme was used to define these levels, yielding more comparable trial counts across categories and accounting for the natural distribution of token-holding states encountered during the task. Neurons were classified as token-holding selective if the $F$-test for the $W$ term reached significance ($\alpha = 0.05$). The preferred token-holding level was defined as the level yielding the smallest $p$-value across pairwise two-sample $t$-tests, each comparing the mean firing rate of one token-holding group against all remaining groups combined. The direction of tuning was determined by the sign of $\bar{r}_{pref} - \bar{r}_{rest}$. For population averaging, the tuning curves of negatively tuned neurons ($\bar{r}_{pref} - \bar{r}_{rest} < 0$) were inverted prior to normalization. Min-max normalization was then applied:
\begin{equation}
    TC_{norm} = \frac{TC - \min(TC)}{\max(TC) - \min(TC)}
\end{equation}
Normalized curves were averaged across neurons sharing the same preferred token-holding level to yield group-level population tuning curves. To characterize the shape of the re-centered population tuning curves (Fig.~\ref{fig:resource_state_tuning}I--K), the normalized firing rates at relative positions $\pm1$ and $\pm2$ from each neuron's preferred token-holding level were extracted and compared using a Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Under the null hypothesis that the tuning curves reflect random noise, firing rates at all non-peak positions would be indistinguishable from one another; a significant difference between positions $\pm1$ and $\pm2$ therefore provides evidence for a genuine, graded decay away from the response peak. This interpretation is corroborated by a shuffle control (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}), in which token-holding group labels were randomly permuted across trials prior to all analyses, confirming that the observed gradient structure cannot be attributed to chance.

For the shuffle control, token-holding-group labels were independently permuted across trials within each unit before repeating unit selection, preferred-group assignment, sign alignment, normalization and peak re-centering. Ten permutations were performed per unit, and their mean selective proportion and peak-aligned curves defined the null estimates. Individual-monkey analyses repeated the complete procedure used for the pooled results (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_resource_tuning}).

\subsubsection{Cross-temporal resource-state decoding}
The cross-temporal procedure described above was applied to resource state across 14 offer-aligned 50-ms bins ($-200$ to 500~ms) and 10 wallet-update-aligned 50-ms bins ($-200$ to 300~ms), producing a $24\times24$ matrix. Resource state was divided into five groups ([1], [2--3], [4--7], [8--15] and [16--24]); 60 test trials were resampled per group. Each matrix entry was compared with a label-shuffled control using a paired one-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test over matched resampling iterations. The complete analysis was repeated separately for each monkey. \textbf{[TODO: specify the observation unit, pairing, included matrix entries, test direction and correction used for the reported cross-region comparisons within the offer-onset quadrant.]}

\subsubsection{Choice-dependent resource-state updating}
For wallet-update trajectories, mean firing rate from $-100$ to 200~ms relative to each update was regressed on $V_E$, $V_S$, earning/spending choice and resource state. The QR order was chosen so that the plotted choice and resource-state axes were residualized against the remaining regressors. \textbf{[TODO: provide the exact QR column order and confirm whether resource state in this regression denotes the pre-update or post-update token count.]}
Test-set population activity was projected from $-100$ to 200~ms onto the resulting axes. Within each resampling iteration, projection onto the resource-state axis was regressed against time separately for earning and spending updates; the temporal coefficient $\beta_{\mathrm{update}}$ was positive for movement toward higher represented wealth and negative for movement toward lower represented wealth. Earning coefficients were tested against zero with a right-tailed Wilcoxon signed-rank test and spending coefficients with a left-tailed test. \subsection{Resource-state modulation of offer-value representations}

\subsubsection{Time-resolved nested-model comparison}
Spike trains were smoothed with the causal Gaussian kernel described above and averaged in non-overlapping 100-ms windows spanning $-200$ to 800~ms relative to offer onset and $-500$ to 500~ms relative to wallet update. The update-aligned analysis was performed both with all valid update events and after randomly subsampling, within unit, to match the number of valid offer-onset trials.

Two nested linear models were fitted in each window. The baseline model ($M_1$) incorporated resource-state-independent offer values, choice direction and 23 wealth-level indicators (wealth 1 as the reference):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3\,\mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k\,\mathbf{1}[W = k].
\end{equation}
where $V_i^{\mathrm{obj}}$ includes the objective earning and spending values derived from the fixed-$\alpha$ behavioral model. The token-holding-modulated model ($M_2$) extended $M_1$ with the free-$\alpha$ earning value as an additional regressor:
\begin{align}
\mathrm{FR} \sim & \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3\,\mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k\,\mathbf{1}[W = k] \\
& + \beta_4\,V_{E,w}.
\end{align}
An analogous increment could not be estimated for resource-dependent spending value. With a fixed spending rate, $V_S$ is the sum of a state-independent reward term and a token-cost term that depends only on $W$; these components were already represented by the spending-value and resource-state regressors in $M_1$, making an additional $V_S$ regressor exactly collinear with the baseline model.
Model performance was quantified by adjusted $R^2$ to penalize the additional parameter in $M_2$. The modulation index $\Delta R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}=R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}(M_2)-R^2_{\mathrm{adj}}(M_1)$ was computed for every unit and time window. The null analysis permuted the state-dependent $V_E$ labels across trials and refitted both models. Observed and shuffled per-unit increments were compared using a paired two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test. \textbf{[TODO: specify the precise within-unit strata used when permuting $V_E$, the number of permutations and correction across time windows.]}

\subsubsection{Resource-state-dependent geometry of offer-value representations}
We used the $V_E$ and $V_S$ TDR axes defined above to visualize the joint population representation of earning and spending offers around resource-state update. The task contained 12 offer conditions formed by crossing three earning rates with four spending reward magnitudes. The zero-reward spending option was rarely chosen and therefore provided too few update-aligned observations for stable condition averages. We excluded this condition from the geometry analysis, leaving the $3\times3$ subset formed by the three earning rates and three non-zero spending rewards. Resource state was divided into low (1 token), middle (2--5 tokens) and high (6--24 tokens) groups. Within each brain area and resource-state group, test-set activity was averaged over $-200$ to $300$~ms relative to resource-state update and then averaged separately for each of the nine offer conditions, yielding nine condition-averaged population states in the $V_E$--$V_S$ space. Lines connecting neighboring conditions in Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}D were included only to display the adjacency structure of the $3\times3$ offer matrix and were not used in any calculation.

To summarize the scale of the projected geometry along each value axis, the nine coordinates on each axis were sorted within every resource-state group and bootstrap iteration. We defined the trimmed earning-axis and spending-axis ranges as
\begin{align}
R_E &= x_{(8)}-x_{(2)},\\
R_S &= y_{(8)}-y_{(2)},
\end{align}
where $x_{(k)}$ and $y_{(k)}$ denote the $k$th ordered coordinates on the $V_E$ and $V_S$ axes, respectively. Excluding the single most extreme coordinate at each end reduced the influence of any one condition mean. Within each resource-state group and bootstrap iteration, the aspect ratio was defined as
\begin{equation}
A=\frac{R_S}{R_E}.
\end{equation}
An aspect ratio greater than 1 indicates that the offer representation extends further along the spending-value axis than along the earning-value axis. Figure~\ref{fig:value_modulation}E shows the mean $\pm$ SD of $A$ across all 100 bootstrap iterations; the component ranges are shown in Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_offer_value_geometry}.

Two shuffle controls were applied to the pooled dataset and repeated the complete regression, cross-validation, projection and geometry procedure. For the value-label shuffle, paired earning--spending offer labels were permuted across trials within each unit and resource-state group, after which $V_E$ and $V_S$ were recomputed from the shuffled offer pair and the original token count. This preserved resource-state composition while disrupting the mapping between offer value and neural activity. For the token-holding-label shuffle, $W$ labels were permuted within each unit and offer condition, after which $V_E$, $V_S$ and the low, middle and high resource-state assignments were recomputed using the shuffled token counts. This preserved offer-condition composition while disrupting the mapping between resource state and neural activity. Each control used 100 bootstrap iterations and the same sampling and best-bootstrap visualization procedures as the unshuffled pooled analysis.

\subsubsection{Joint-encoding analysis}
To characterize the co-representation of offer value and token holdings, the following regression was fitted separately to each unit's mean unsmoothed firing rate during offer onset (0--500~ms) and wallet update ($-300$ to 300~ms):
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i^{\mathrm{obj}} + \beta_3\,\mathrm{Choice} + \beta_4\,W,
\end{equation}
where $W$ was entered as a continuous linear predictor and objective (fixed-$\alpha$) values were used to ensure that its coefficient captured token-holding-related firing variance orthogonal to value encoding. A unit was classified as positively or negatively selective for a given variable if the corresponding regression coefficient was significant ($p < 0.05$); otherwise it was excluded from the contingency for that variable pair. Only units simultaneously selective for both value and token holdings were entered into the $2\times2$ contingency table. For earning value, a left-tailed Fisher's exact test was applied, based on the a priori directional prediction that diminishing marginal utility produces an anti-diagonal pattern (VE$+$/Token$-$). For spending value, a right-tailed Fisher's exact test was used, reflecting the complementary prediction that higher token holdings increase subjective spending value, producing a diagonal pattern (VS$+$/Token$+$).

\subsection{Decision-related value and choice representations}
\subsubsection{Single-unit good-based and action-based choice selectivity}
For each neuron, firing rates were averaged over the offer period (0--500~ms post-onset) to yield a single response scalar per trial. These mean rates were regressed against the monkey's binary choice in each domain:
\begin{equation}
FR \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 \cdot Choice_{LR} + \beta_2 \cdot Choice_{ES}.
\end{equation}
Coefficient significance classified units as selective in the action and/or good-based domains (Supplementary Fig.~\ref{fig:supp_choice_units}). Relative encoding was quantified from $|\beta_{ES}|$ and $|\beta_{LR}|$. For each unit, $d=(|\beta_{ES}|-|\beta_{LR}|)/\sqrt{2}$ measured displacement along the anti-diagonal of the coefficient plane. A two-sided binomial test on the sign of $d$ tested whether an area contained more units biased toward one reference frame than expected under a 0.5 null proportion. Spearman rank correlation quantified the association between $|\beta_{ES}|$ and $|\beta_{LR}|$ across units.

\subsubsection{Joint encoding of $V_E$ and $V_S$}
To test whether single units preferentially encoded the value difference $\Delta V = V_E - V_S$, we fit an OLS regression model to mean firing rates in the offer-onset epoch ($[0, 500]$~ms) and the token-update epoch ($[0, 150]$~ms):
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \beta_1 V_E + \beta_2 V_S + \beta_3 \, \mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k \, \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{token} = k],
\end{equation*}
where token holdings were included as a 23-level dummy-coded categorical variable (1 token as reference). A unit was classified as $V_E$-positive ($V_{E,\mathrm{pos}}$) if $\beta_1 > 0$ and $p(\beta_1) < 0.05$, as $V_E$-negative ($V_{E,\mathrm{neg}}$) if $\beta_1 < 0$ and $p(\beta_1) < 0.05$, and non-selective otherwise; the analogous classification was applied to $\beta_2$ for $V_S$. Among units significant for both $V_E$ and $V_S$, we constructed a $2 \times 2$ contingency table (rows: $V_E$ sign; columns: $V_S$ sign) and applied Fisher's exact test to assess whether the anti-diagonal cells ($V_{E,\mathrm{pos}} \times V_{S,\mathrm{neg}}$ and $V_{E,\mathrm{neg}} \times V_{S,\mathrm{pos}}$) were over-represented, as would be expected if $\Delta V$ were the dominant encoded quantity.

\subsubsection{Signed chosen-advantage selectivity}
The behavioral model includes a bias parameter $b$ representing the animal's indifference point between earning and spending. This parameter enters the choice rule as a shift on $V_E-V_S$ and cannot be attributed to either offer independently. We therefore defined signed chosen advantage, denoted throughout by $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$,
\begin{equation*}
    V_{\mathrm{Chosen}} = (V_E - V_S - b) \times c,
\end{equation*}
where $c=+1$ for earning choices and $c=-1$ for spending choices. This variable is the signed value advantage of the chosen option over the unchosen option relative to the fitted indifference point; it is not the absolute subjective value of the selected offer. The regression model was
\begin{equation*}
    \mathrm{FR} \sim \beta_0 + \sum_i\beta_i V_i + \beta_4 \, \mathrm{Choice} + \sum_{k=2}^{24} \gamma_k \, \mathbf{1}[\mathrm{token} = k],
\end{equation*}
where $V_i\in\{V_E,V_S,V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}\}$, fit to mean unsmoothed firing rates in the offer-onset epoch (0--500~ms). Token holdings were represented by 23 indicators for holdings 2--24, with 1 token as the reference. Units with $p(\beta_{V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}})<0.05$ were classified as signed-chosen-advantage selective. The empirical false-positive proportion was estimated from 10 repetitions in which trial labels were randomly permuted before refitting the complete model. \textbf{[TODO: specify which labels were permuted jointly and whether permutation was restricted within unit or other trial strata.]}

\subsubsection{Choice-conditioned value encoding}
To test whether the represented value depended on the selected option, trials were divided into earning and spending choices and independent TDR models were fitted within each pool:
\begin{equation}
FR\sim\beta_0+\beta_E V_E+\beta_S V_S+\beta_W W.
\end{equation}
The regression window was 0--500~ms after offer onset and the projection window was $-200$ to 500~ms. QR ordering was $[W,V_E,V_S]$. Test trials were divided into tertiles of the value being decoded, with 100 trials resampled per tertile. Choice dependence was summarized by
\begin{equation}
D=[R^2(V_E\mid Earn)-R^2(V_S\mid Earn)]-[R^2(V_E\mid Spend)-R^2(V_S\mid Spend)].
\end{equation}

Temporal encoding of $V_E$, $V_S$, $V_{\mathrm{Chosen}}$ and categorical earning/spending choice was estimated using the joint regression described in the TDR section, repeating QR with each target placed last.
